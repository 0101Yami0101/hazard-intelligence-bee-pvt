1The whole idea in one picture

An app is a vehicle. The backbone is the road. We build the road, then run vehicles on it.

💡 Lead with the road, not a vehicle. We are not "a landslide app". The forecasts are the proof that the road carries weight.
🎮 the game version

Think of any video game. The world (the ground, the map, the weather) is built once by the game engine. Then lots of characters play on that same world. Nobody builds a brand-new world for every new character.

That's us. We built the World Engine (the Data Backbone). Three Guardians play on it: SlideSense, FloodSense and SoilSense. There's also a 🔒 empty slot for a fourth Guardian later ("next hazard?").

The chests that feed the world come from five places: sky-eyes (satellites), guild halls (government websites), river height sticks (gauges), the sky (live weather) and one 🔒 locked chest, our own sensors, which aren't built yet.

The road and vehicles above are the same idea. Lead with the road (the world engine), not one vehicle (one Guardian). The forecasts prove the road works.
A gamer says "Cool, you made a landslide game!" What do you say?
"Not quite. We built the world engine. The landslide Guardian is just the first character, there to show the world works. A flood Guardian and a soil Guardian already play on it too."
🗣️ Say what it is in one breath: "A living, forecast-ready data backbone: every source checked, lined up on one map, updated daily, and graded against what really happens." The kinds of data, in standard words: topographic, geological, pedological (soil), land cover, hydrological, meteorological, seismic, cryospheric, hazard event records, and exposure. Use "geospatial", not "environmental"; never call it a "spatial data infrastructure" to APSAC (that's APSSDI). The full version, the word choices and APSSDI vs us: Phase 2, section 0.

3The problem: four walls

Every hazard team hits the same four walls. The backbone knocks down all four, once.

🧩
Data in piecesHeight every 30 m, soil every 250 m, rain every 11 km, rock as outlines, rivers as lines. A dozen agencies.Bug: mismatched blocks. Like LEGO from five different sets: tiny bricks, chunky bricks, giant slabs, shapes and strings. They don't snap together, and they come from a dozen different shops.
🔁
Every project starts from zeroMonths lining data up, one map published, the work thrown away.Bug: deleted save file. Each team spends months building the world, plays one round, then deletes the save. The next team starts from nothing.
🥀
Maps go staleGround barely changes. Rain and rivers change every day.Bug: a frozen screenshot. A printed map is a screenshot. The mountains in it are fine, since they hardly move. But the rain and rivers are frozen too, and in real life they change every day.
📋
Nobody keeps scoreForecasts are never written down and checked. So nothing learns.Bug: no scoreboard. Players shout guesses ("it'll flood tomorrow!") but nobody writes them down or checks them. So nobody gets better, and nobody can prove they're good.
🔑 How we learned this: building the first landslide forecast, the model was the small part. Finding, checking and lining up the data was most of the work, and all of it could be reused.
🎮 why the bugs matter

When we built our first Guardian, its brain (the model) took only a small part of the time. Most of the time went on the loot run: finding chests, scanning them for glitches, and snapping the pieces onto one grid. That work is the same for every Guardian. So we kept it, and turned it into the World Engine.

The four walls are pieces, starting from zero, stale maps and no score. The backbone fixes all four, once. The model was the small part; the data work was most of it, and it is reusable.
Name the four bugs in 10 seconds.
Mismatched blocks (data in pieces) · deleted save file (every project starts from zero) · frozen screenshot (maps go stale) · no scoreboard (nobody keeps score).

🎮 the World Engine's conveyor belt
  • 🎁 Loot run (collect): every day, little bots go out and open chests: satellites, government websites, river height sticks, the weather.
  • 🔐 The Vault (keep): each item goes in untouched, with a tag saying where it was found, the rules for using it, the link and the date. It also gets a fingerprint: a code that changes if even one tiny bit of the file changes, so you can always tell if something was altered.
  • 🧲 Snap to grid (line up): every item is reshaped to fit the same blocks. A giant rain slab is painted onto every block under it. A rock shape becomes "which rock am I on?". A river string becomes "how far is the nearest river?".
  • 📦 Two chests (shelve): the Materials chest holds ready-to-use stuff, the same for everyone. The Answers chest holds the Guardians' predictions.
  • 🛡️ Guardians use it (serve): they grab what they need from the chests and show you the answer on screen.
  • ↩ The loop: every guess is written in the Prophecy Book before the day. After the day the Scoreboard checks it. What really happened becomes new XP, and the Guardians learn from it.
Collect → keep → line up → shelve → serve, plus the loop: forecasts are written down before the day, graded after it, and what happened comes back as new data. Click each step above for its folder and whether it's built.
Why must the guess go in the Prophecy Book before the day?
Otherwise anyone could say afterwards "I knew it!". Writing it down first keeps the check honest. And a forecast nobody wrote down is gone forever, so it can never be checked or learned from.

The state is cut into 8.2 million squares of 100 m × 100 m, each about the size of a football field. Every source becomes one more layer over the same squares. Pick a square and read straight down.

solid = uses it today · dashed = planned · grey = not used
🧱 One file, three products. The ground-height file, the DEM (Digital Elevation Model: Copernicus GLO-30, one height every 30 m), comes in as 28 satellite tiles. The backbone turns it into steepness, slope facing, drainage and height above the river. SlideSense reads the steepness. FloodSense reads the height above the river. SoilSense reads the slope again.
🎮 open a block's inventory

Pick any block in Blockland and open its inventory. Inside are 34 items (facts): how steep it is, which way it faces, what rock and soil it has, forest or bare ground, how high it sits above its river, how far to the nearest road or fault crack, and more. Each block also has a rain diary: 26 years of rain, every single day.

It's like a layer cake. Cut straight down through one block and you see every layer stacked up: weather on top, ground shape at the bottom. Every block's inventory has the same slots, so every Guardian knows exactly where to look.

How big is 8.2 million? If you visited one block every second, day and night with no sleep, it would take you about 95 days to see them all.

One chest, three uses. The ground-height chest (grown-ups call it the DEM) arrives as 28 jigsaw tiles from a satellite. The engine turns it into steepness, facing, drainage and height above the river, like one bag of flour making bread, cake and rotis. SlideSense reads the steepness, FloodSense the height above the river, and SoilSense the slope again.

8.2 million squares of 100 m, 34 facts each, plus 26 years of daily rain = shelf one, the same for every product. The 28-tile height file feeds all three products.
Why put every chest onto the same blocks?
Two reasons. A Guardian's brain reads a table: one row per block, one column per fact. And two Guardians can then never argue about where a place is, because they share the very same blocks.

6The six rules (plus three the pitch adds)

These rules are why anyone should trust what comes out. Learn each with its "why".

1
One map for everythinga model needs a table, and two products can never disagree about where a place isGamerule: one world for everyone. If two Guardians used different maps, one might say "the village is here" and the other "no, it's over there". Same blocks, no arguments.
🔍 Why it matters: 10 things one map makes possible

📐 Think of graph paper. If 50 students each draw the school on their own paper, at their own size, you can't lay the maps on top of each other. If everyone draws on the same numbered squares, you just say "square B7" and every map agrees.

  1. A model can read it. One row per square, one column per fact: a 30 m height map, a 250 m soil map and a rock drawing become 34 columns of one table.
  2. Products never argue about where. "Square 4,512,880" means the same ground to every Guardian.
  3. Hazards can be combined. "Slide-prone and flood-prone" is a lookup, not a project.
  4. Add a source once, every product gets it. New data = one more column, for everyone.
  5. Checking is easy. A reported slide lands on one square: "what did we forecast here that day?" Our map and GSI's can be compared place by place.
  6. "Who's at risk?" is a lookup. Villages, roads and hospitals sit on the same squares as the hazards.
  7. Change over time works. The same square in 2019 and 2025 can be compared directly (SoilSense's bare ground each dry season).
  8. The app stays small and fast. It looks up a square by number instead of doing heavy map maths.
  9. It moves to a new state. The same grid rules gave Assam its own grid.
  10. Partners can share without confusion. A square ID works like a PIN code.

🗣️ Say: "Everything sits on one grid, so any two facts, any two products, or a forecast and its outcome can be compared square by square, with no arguments about where."

2
Keep the original, record where it came fromanyone can audit a number. If a source dies we lose freshness, not the systemGamerule: never edit loot, always tag it. Like a museum that keeps the original painting with a label. Anyone can check where a number came from. If a shop closes, we stop getting fresh items, but the game keeps running.
3
Check a source before trusting itofficial data can be wrong: the official flood map put most "flooded" ground on steep slopesGamerule: scan every chest for glitches. One official flood map showed water sitting on steep cliffs, like a puddle stuck to a wall. Water can't do that! So we test a chest before we trust it.
4
The backbone doesn't know the hazardnothing in it says "landslide" or "flood", which is what makes the next product cheapGamerule: the engine stays neutral. It only knows ground, rain and rivers, like LEGO bricks that don't know whether they'll become a castle or a spaceship. That's why a new Guardian is cheap to add.
5
Two shelvesshelf 1 is ready-to-use data (a road planner wants this). Shelf 2 is the answers (an officer wants this)Gamerule: two chests. A road builder wants the Materials chest (the raw blocks). A district officer wants the Answers chest ("is it dangerous this week?").
6
Close the loopmore data makes a forecast fresher. Only knowing what happened makes it betterGamerule: always check the prophecy. More loot keeps the game fresh. Only checking your guesses against what really happened makes you better, like a cricketer watching the replay of how they got out.
🧪 Test on unseen ground. A model is judged only on places it never saw during building.No peeking. A surprise exam with new questions, not the practice paper you memorised.
🏷️ Label every number. Each number on screen says what it is: a forecast, a measurement or a record.Every number wears a badge: a guess about tomorrow, a ruler reading, or a page from the history book.
🤚 Say so when unsure. Where data can't support an answer, the app says so and doesn't guess.When Pixel doesn't know, Pixel says "I don't know", instead of making something up.
🎮 why gamerules matter

A game with no rules is chaos: nobody trusts the score. These nine rules are why an expert can trust what the Guardians say. Learn each rule with its "why", because experts always ask "why?".

The six rules: one map, keep originals with a record, check sources, a hazard-neutral backbone, two shelves, close the loop. Plus three from the pitch: test on unseen ground, label every number, say so when unsure.
Which rule stops a Guardian from cheating in its exam?
Test on unseen ground. It's only marked on places it never saw while training, so it can't just remember the answers.

🔫 The loaded gun. A weak slope is a loaded gun (WHERE). Heavy rain pulls the trigger (WHEN). You need both. That's why a "risk map" alone is not the product: it says nothing about today.

🗣️ Vocabulary: scientists call WHERE "susceptibility" and WHEN "trigger". To clients we only say "Landslide Forecast" and "Flood Forecast". Susceptibility is an ingredient, not the meal.
🎮 TNT × spark: the most important idea in the game

In Blockland, a weak hill block is a 🧨 TNT block. TNT doesn't explode by itself. It just sits there, sometimes for years. It needs a ⚡ spark: heavy rain, or a rising river. TNT + spark = boom. TNT with no spark is calm today. A spark on solid stone does nothing.

A map of all the TNT blocks (a "risk map") is useful, but it was just as true last year. It can't tell you whether there's a spark this week. That's why the Guardians make a forecast: the TNT map × this week's sparks, for the next 7 days.

Grown-up words: the TNT map is called susceptibility, and the spark is called the trigger. Customers only hear "Landslide Forecast" and "Flood Forecast". Susceptibility is like flour: an ingredient, not the cake.

WHERE (the ground, barely changes) × WHEN (rain and river, change daily) = a forecast for the next 7 days. It's the loaded gun and the trigger. A risk map alone is not the product. Tap the three tabs above to see each Guardian's TNT and spark.
A friend shows you a 2015 map of dangerous hills and says "we already have a forecast". What's missing?
The spark. That map only shows the TNT blocks (WHERE). It says nothing about this week's rain or river (WHEN), so it can't tell you which day to worry about.

9The numbers card

👆 Tap a card to flip it. Say what it means before you flip. 🎮 The back also shows the stat in game words.

🎮 the stats screen

Every game has a stats screen. Pros know their stats by heart, and what each stat doesn't mean. A stat said wrong is worse than no stat at all.

"6 in 7" and "20% = 20%": which one is a chance, and which one isn't?
20% = 20% is about chances: at a gauged river, of all the days it said 20%, about 1 in 5 really reached the line (honest dice). 6 in 7 is not a chance. It's how often the landslide map ranks a real slide's block above a quiet block it has never seen.

10Don't say → say instead

Each wrong phrase on the left has been tempting before. Experts catch every one.

✗ don't say
✓ say instead
"We are a landslide app."
"We build the data backbone. The forecasts are the proof."
Saying "we made one character" when you built the whole world engine.
"It is 86% accurate."
"Landslide WHERE picks the right spot about 6 times in 7, on ground it never saw."
"86% accurate" is a made-up stat. The real stat: show it a slide block and a quiet block it never saw, and 6 times in 7 it picks the right one.
"High means a 70% chance of a landslide."
"A landslide level is a rank: High means look here first. High has seen about 3× the usual landslides."
A leaderboard spot isn't a percent. "Top of the table" doesn't mean "70% sure to win".
"We caught a real flood."
"Not yet. It was tested hard on past years. The next monsoon is the proof."
We've beaten the practice levels (past years). The real boss fight is the next monsoon.
"The loop is closed."
"Closed for floods. Landslides are written down daily but not graded yet."
Only FloodSense's prophecies get marked so far.
"We own unique data."
"Most inputs are public. Ours is the assembly, the upkeep and the checked history."
Anyone can open the same chests. Our skill is building the world from them, keeping it fresh, and the scoreboard history.
"It works for any state."
"It moved to Assam in about four weeks. Each new state needs its own records and checks."
The engine can load a new world (Assam took about a month), but every world needs its own chests and its own tests.
"Others build on our feed. We send SMS alerts."
"Planned." (Some pitch slides show planned things. Say so.)
Locked items. Say "coming in a future update".
🎮 cheat codes get you banned

Use a cheat code in an online game and you get banned. Say a too-good number to an expert and you get "banned" too: they stop trusting everything else you say. Honest moves win the long game.

Someone says "So High means 70% chance, right?" Your honest move?
"No. High is a rank: look here first. On past days, High places saw about 3 times the usual number of landslides. It isn't a percentage."
