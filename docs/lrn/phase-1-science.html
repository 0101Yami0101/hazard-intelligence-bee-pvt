2Why slopes fail

A slope is a tug of war. Gravity pulls the soil downhill. Grip (friction plus roots and cement) holds it. When pull beats grip, it slides.

A hill stays put while its grip is stronger than gravity's pull. Rain sneaks into the soil and makes the grip weaker. When pull wins, the hill slides.

🎮 why blocks fall: the tug of war

Every hill block is in a 🤼 tug of war. Pull (gravity) drags it downhill. Grip (friction between grains, plus tree roots) holds it in place. A steeper hill means a stronger pull. More roots mean more grip.

Now the Monsoon arrives. Rain soaks in and fills the tiny gaps between the soil grains. The water pushes the grains apart, so they rub less, and the grip drops. The hill didn't get steeper. The water made it weaker. When pull wins, the block slides.

That's why the 🧨 TNT and the ⚡ spark are two different things. The TNT is ground that is steep, cracked or cut. The spark is the water that tips it over.

Factor of safety = grip ÷ pull; below 1 the slope fails. Water raises the pressure between grains (pore pressure), which cuts friction. Faults, clay-forming rock and road cuts make the ground weaker. We map WHERE with tree models (LightGBM) trained on 35,744 mapped slides, not with the physics sum, because the soil depth and strength it needs aren't measured across the state.
A hill has stood for 100 years. Why could it fall this week?
The hill didn't change; the water did. Heavy rain fills the gaps between soil grains and pushes them apart, so grip drops below gravity's pull. Push the 💧 slider up in the toy above and watch it happen.
Why don't we just do the physics sum for every block?
The sum needs the soil's depth and strength at every block, and nobody has measured those across Arunachal. So we learn the pattern from 35,744 real slides instead. The toy above only builds the feeling.

3Rain as the trigger

The classic way to forecast landslides from rain is a threshold line. We tried it four ways, measured why it fails here, and replaced it with a sliding score. This section is the full story, with every number re-checked against the data.

Weak ground needs a spark, and the main spark is rain. The textbook spark detector (a "rain line") rings on most monsoon days here, so it's useless as an alarm. So we ask instead: is this rain unusual for this exact block?

The textbook idea (Caine 1980, Guzzetti 2008): plot past landslides by how long it rained and how hard. Draw the lowest line under them. Rain above the line = "slides can start".

I = a · D−b

I = how hard (mm per hour), D = how long (hours). The minus sign is the whole idea: long storms trigger at lighter rain, short ones need a downpour.

Two published lines are in our code (rainfall_features.py): Caine 1980 I = 14.82·D−0.39 and Guzzetti 2008 (global) I = 2.20·D−0.44.

The textbook spark detector. Draw a line on a chart, just under every old slide. If today's rain lands above the line, ring the alarm. Long, gentle rain can ring it; so can a short, hard downpour.

The trigger, step by step, as it runs every day

1Fetch rain for the last days + the next 7 at 219 points (Open-Meteo).
2Add up 3-day and 7-day totals for each day.
3Look each total up in that point's own May–October history since 2010 (stored as 101 cut-points, so the app's file is tiny).
4Average the two percentiles → rain score 0–1.
5Multiply by the ground score (Phase 3) → the level on the map.

Designed and scored on NASA's 11 km satellite rain (2000–2026). The live app uses Open-Meteo's history, so the backtest re-scored the live version separately. Code: scripts/model/trigger.py, webapp/core/rainfall.py.

Every day (the ⏰ 8:30 alarm, Level 6): fetch the rain → add up the last 3 and 7 days → look each total up in that block's rain diary → average the two → multiply by the TNT score → paint the map.
🎮 the spark detector

The TNT map says which blocks are weak. But weak blocks sit there for years. We need a spark detector that says "today is the dangerous day".

The textbook detector is a line on a chart. Here it acts like a smoke alarm that rings whenever you make toast: about 3 in every 4 ordinary rainy monsoon days already sit above the line. An alarm that rings all the time is no alarm.

So SlideSense asks a smarter question: "Is this rain strange for this block?" Each block opens its own 📔 rain diary (its May–October rain since 2010), looks at the last 3 days and the last 7 days, and asks "how many diary days were wetter than this?". The two answers are averaged into a spark score from 0 to 1.

Rain score = the average of the 3-day and 7-day percentiles against each point's own monsoon history (219 points, Open-Meteo, live). Nothing fitted. It ranks slide days above ordinary days 0.768 of the time on 72 dated slides (range about 0.70–0.82), and 0.69 on 583 Himalayan slides. The classic threshold line is kept only as the physics explanation.
The spark score says 0.97. Is there a 97% chance of a landslide?
No. It means the last few days were wetter than about 97% of monsoon days in that place's own diary. It measures how unusual the rain is, not the chance of a slide. The ground (the TNT) still has to be weak.
Why not use one rain number for the whole state, like "80 mm in 3 days = danger"?
Because the state ranges from about 800 to 4,200 mm of rain a year. One number rings about 32 days a monsoon in the wettest places and about 5 in the driest. Comparing each place with its own diary gives each about 18: fair everywhere.

🎮 Blockland: one TNT block, many kinds of spark

Remember: a weak block is a 🧨 TNT block, and something has to light it. Rain is the spark we watch every day. But Blockland has other sparks too:

  • ⚡ Rain spark: the one we watch today, for all three Guardians.
  • 🌊 River-rising spark and 💦 full-sponge spark (soaked ground): FloodSense watches these too.
  • 📳 Quake shake: the whole world jolts and TNT blocks tumble. Next on SlideSense's list; its chest is already in the Vault.
  • ❄️ Melting spark: snow melting on the high blocks. Its chest is spotted, not opened.
  • 🧊 Ice-dam burst: a lake up in the ice breaks its wall. That's a whole new Guardian one day, not a spark for today's ones.
  • 🚰 Dam tap: a person opens a dam. We'd need the dam keepers to tell us their plans.
  • ⛏️ Digger spark: road cutting and blasting. The cut slope stays weaker afterwards, so the TNT itself grows.
  • ⛈️ Pinpoint spark: a cloudburst so small and quick that our rain map can't see it. Only a radar or a sensor nearby can.
  • 🔥 Fire doesn't light the TNT. It burns off the TNT's wrapping (the roots), so the next rain spark hits harder.
Live: rain (all), river level and soil wetness (floods). Planned: earthquake shaking (slides), sensors. Ready but unused: snowmelt, fire. Needs an ask: dam releases, blasting records, glacial-lake maps. Gap: cloudbursts.
Is a road cut a trigger or a condition?
Mostly a condition: it leaves the slope weaker, so it belongs to WHERE (that's why "distance to road" is one of the 34 facts). The day of the digging or blasting is a trigger, and those records are on our ask list.
Why can't our forecast see a cloudburst coming?
It's too small and too short. Our rain data comes in squares of about 11 km (history) and 22 km (live), a day at a time, and a cloudburst can hit one valley for an hour. Warning by the hour needs weather radar or a sensor upstream. What we can say is "this valley is primed this week".

4Water on a height map

From a grid of ground heights alone, you can work out where every drop of rain goes. Step through it. The numbers are a tiny made-up valley.

Rain always runs to the lowest block next door. Follow every drop and you find the streams. Blocks that sit only a little higher than a stream flood first.

⚪ The marble run. Put a marble on every block. Each marble rolls to its lowest neighbour (step 2). Count how many marbles roll through each block (step 3). Where lots of marbles pass, there's a stream (step 4). Then measure how high each block sits above its stream (step 5): that's HAND.
🌊 HAND = Height Above Nearest Drainage (Nobre et al. 2011). A square 2 m above its stream floods long before one 40 m above it. It is the single strongest clue to where floods reach.⬆️ Steps above the river. HAND is how many metres you'd climb from the nearest stream up to this block. 2 m is one big step: water reaches it easily. 40 m is a whole building: water can't.
🗺️ How FloodSense uses it: its WHERE half reads only shape: height above the river, river size and how closed-in the valley is. Adding land cover made test scores higher but the map wrong (see trap 1 below).Shape, not paint. FloodSense's TNT map reads only the shape of the ground. When we let it read land cover too, its score went up, but it called mountain ice lakes the most flood-prone places: a 👾 Glitch (section 9).
🎮 where water goes

Water is lazy. It always goes downhill, to the lowest block next door. That's the whole rule. From the ⛰️ ground-height chest alone (Level 0), the computer follows every drop and finds every stream and every valley.

Then it asks one question for every block: "How high am I above the stream I drain into?" That's HAND. A house 2 m above the river gets wet in a normal flood. A house 40 m up the hillside stays dry, even if it's just as close to the river.

Height map (Copernicus DEM, 30 m) → flow direction (D8: each square drains to its steepest-down neighbour) → flow accumulation → streams and catchments → HAND (Nobre et al. 2011). FloodSense's WHERE half reads only shape: HAND, river size, and how closed-in the valley is.
Two houses are the same distance from a river. Why can one flood and the other stay dry?
Distance isn't height. The one that sits only a little above the river (low HAND) floods first. The one high up the slope (high HAND) stays dry. That's why FloodSense reads height above the river, not just distance.

5River forecasting

A gauge measures how high the river is. Officials draw lines on that scale. A flood forecast asks: will the river reach the line, and when?

Each river has a height stick with lines painted on it. The big question: will the water reach the red line, and when?

🏛️ CWC (Central Water Commission) runs the main gauges and sets danger levels. Where none exists, our line is the river's own wettest 2% of days.The River Guild paints the lines. Where they haven't painted one, we paint our own at the height the river reaches only on its wettest 2 days in 100.
⏱️ Lead time = how far ahead. Big slow rivers give days of warning; small hill streams rise in hours. That's physics, not budget.🛁 Bathtub vs cup. A big river is a huge bathtub: it takes days to fill, so you see it coming. A hill stream is a cup under a tap: full in minutes. No money changes that.
🎲 Our river forecast gives a chance the river reaches its line in the next 7 days, from rain plus the river's own recent week. A dotted level line goes 2 days ahead.🎲 Honest dice. At rivers with a height stick, the chance is honest: on the days it said about 15%, the river reached its line about 15% of the time. More in section 8 and Level 4.

Reads the gauge's last week plus upstream gauges. Forecasts level 8–48 h ahead, with about 0.1 m error at 24 h. Works best on big, slow rivers, not flash floods.
Another player: Google. One of its models guesses river flow from the weather anywhere in the world, without looking at the river's own height stick. The other reads the last week of height sticks, for 8–48 hours ahead.
🧭 Our stance: we build our own models on Arunachal's rivers, so we can prove them to clients. Google's model can be a cross-check on the big rivers, not our feed. 🎮 the river's height stick

Picture a tall ruler standing in the river: a 📏 height stick (a gauge). The water level on it is read every day. The River Guild (CWC) has painted lines on it: a warning line, a danger line, and a mark for the highest the water has ever been.

FloodSense asks: "How likely is the water to reach the danger line?" It looks at the rain coming and at how the river itself behaved this past week. For 17 rivers it also draws a dotted line showing where the level is heading, 2 days ahead.

A gauge measures water level. CWC sets warning and danger levels and records the highest flood level (HFL). Where no danger level exists, we use the river's own wettest 2% of days. FloodSense's river chance is calibrated at gauged rivers; the dotted level line runs 2 days ahead on 17 CWC gauges. Lead time depends on river size: physics, not budget.
Why does a hill stream give less warning than a big river?
A big river is a bathtub: it fills over days, so the rise shows early. A small hill stream is a cup under a tap: it rises in hours. That limit is physics. Money can't change it, though a sensor upstream can buy a little time.
Google already forecasts floods. Why build our own?
So we can prove it works on Arunachal's own rivers, with our own scores, and fix it ourselves. Google's model can be a cross-check on the big rivers.

6Soil loss: the five-factor recipe (RUSLE)

The standard soil-conservation sum (Renard et al. 1997). Five numbers multiplied together give tonnes of soil lost per hectare per year.

Soil loss is a recipe of five things multiplied: rain strength × soil type × slope × ground cover × protection. If any one of them is near zero, the answer is near zero.

Values are round, illustrative numbers for learning, not SoilSense outputs.

✖️ Multiplied, not added. If any one factor is near zero (flat ground, thick forest), the loss is near zero. Same logic as WHERE × WHEN.🍰 A cake with no flour. Multiply anything by zero and you get zero. Thick forest makes cover nearly zero, so even heavy rain on a steep slope loses little soil.
🧱 "If untreated". P (bunds, terraces) is unknown until the department shares its treated-area records, so we set P = 1. Every SoilSense number means "if nothing has been done here".We don't know where the walls are. Farmers build small walls and steps (bunds, terraces) that hold soil. Until the department shares where they are, we assume none.
⚠️ Trap we caught: India's common rain formula (Singh 1981) read 91% too low at Guwahati against a real gauge. We use a satellite rain-strength map (InRESatE) instead.👾 A broken ruler. India's usual rain-strength formula read 91% too low when we checked it against a real rain gauge at Guwahati. So we swapped in a satellite rain-strength map.
🎮 the second world's recipe (Assam)

In world 2 (Assam), Guardian 3, SoilSense, watches soil wash away, like waves slowly eating a sandcastle. Its rule is a recipe with five ingredients, multiplied:

🌧️ rain × 🟫 soil × ⛰️ slope × 🌳 cover × 🧱 protection

Try the presets above. A 🔥 bare jhum slope loses a lot. The 🌲 forest hill, with the same rain and slope, loses very little. The same jhum slope with 🧱 terraces loses a fifth as much.

RUSLE: A = R × K × LS × C × P, in tonnes per hectare per year (Renard et al. 1997). P = 1 ("if untreated") until treated-area records are shared. R comes from a satellite erosivity map (InRESatE), because the common Singh 1981 formula read 91% too low at Guwahati.
A steep hill gets heavy rain, but it's covered in thick forest. Why is the soil loss small?
Because the five factors are multiplied. Thick forest makes the cover factor C tiny (about 0.003 in the toy), so the whole answer becomes tiny, however big the rain and slope are.

🎮 the ☁️ Cloud Curtain

Every June the Monsoon boss pulls the ☁️ Cloud Curtain across the sky. 📷 Camera-Eye (optical) can't see through it, so for months it sees only cloud. That's exactly when floods and slides happen.

📡 Echo-Eye (radar) doesn't need sunlight. It sends its own signal down and listens for the bounce. Rough ground bounces it back (bright). Calm water bounces it away, like a mirror (dark). So radar is our flood checker in the monsoon.

Sentinel-1 (radar, ~10 m) sees through cloud and at night; water looks dark (low backscatter); its blind spot is radar shadow in steep valleys. Sentinel-2 (optical, 10 m) is cloud-blocked in the monsoon. IMERG rain is ~11 km, the DEM is 30 m, the map is 100 m: about 12,000 map squares share one rain value.
Why does a flood show up dark on radar?
Calm water is smooth, like a mirror. It bounces the radar signal away from the satellite instead of back, so little echo returns and the water looks dark.
Our map has 100 m blocks. Is the rain that sharp?
No. One rain value covers about 11 km, roughly 12,000 blocks. The sharp detail on the map comes from the ground (the TNT), not from the weather.

8Is the model any good? Four ideas

This is where reviewers attack. Learn these four, and the traps in section 9.

Four exam rules for any Guardian. Does it rank the right blocks first? Is its chance the right size? Was it tested on blocks it never saw? And don't be fooled by "the tallest of 97".

A · AUC: "pick a pair"

Take one random square that had a slide and one that didn't. Did the model score the slide square higher? AUC = how often it does. 0.5 = coin toss. 1.0 = perfect.

running AUC
–
SlideSense WHERE: 0.859 on unseen ground ≈ "6 times in 7"
The pick-the-right-block game. Show the Guardian one block that slid and one that didn't. Which one does it point at? Play many rounds. SlideSense picks right about 6 times in 7 (0.859). It says nothing about how big the chance is: that's the next rule.

B · Calibration: "does 20% mean 20%?"

A model can rank well (good AUC) and still say the wrong size of chance. Calibrated means: of all the days it said 20%, about 20% came true.

✅ FloodSense's river chances are calibrated: at a gauged river, 20% means about 20%.

⚠️ SlideSense levels are not chances. They are ranks, with a measured "× the usual".

Base rate: how common the event is anyway. If floods happen on 1% of days, "5%" is already 5× the usual.

🎲 Honest dice. Think of a weather reporter who says "20% chance of rain". Watch them for a year. If on their "20%" days it really rained on about 1 day in 5, they're honest. That's calibrated. A model can rank well and still be wrong about the size, like a friend who always picks the winning team but always says "100% sure!".

C · Test on ground it has never seen (spatial cross-validation)

random split ✗
block split ✓

■ train   ■ test. Neighbouring squares are nearly identical. In a random split, every test square has a twin in training, so the score is too good (often 0.90+ in papers).

We hold out whole blocks of the state, 5 times over. Score: 0.859 ± 0.016. Honest, but lower than random-split papers. Not comparable: always ask which split a paper used.

Leakage = any way the answer sneaks into training. A random split is the most common kind.

🏋️ The surprise exam. Neighbouring blocks are almost twins. If the exam uses blocks picked at random, every exam block has a twin the Guardian already studied: that's 👀 copying from your twin. So we hide whole regions of the map, five times over, and test there. Our 0.859 comes from that harder exam, so don't compare it with a paper's 0.95 from an easy one.

D · A percentile max looks like 99% on a normal day

Take 97 rain points. Each one's percentile is a fair random draw on an ordinary day. What is the highest of the 97?

The old app showed this max as "0.99" for every town, every day. It looked like "99% chance of a landslide". It was just arithmetic. Now: show each place's own value, or the share of land at High.
The tallest of 97 kids. Line up 97 kids from any school. The tallest one is always tall. That doesn't mean there's a giant in the school. In the same way, the wettest of 97 rain points always looks like "99%", even on a normal day.
🎮 the four exam rules
  1. Rank right (AUC): does it point at the dangerous block first? 0.5 = coin toss, 1 = perfect. SlideSense's TNT map: 0.859.
  2. Size right (calibration): when it says 20%, does it happen about 20% of the time? So far only our gauged-river chance has passed.
  3. Surprise exam (block testing): scored only on regions it never saw while learning.
  4. Don't be fooled by the tallest: the biggest of many random numbers always looks scary.
AUC measures ranking (discrimination). Calibration measures whether the size of a chance is right. Spatial block cross-validation stops leakage from near-identical neighbours. The max of many percentiles is near 1 by arithmetic alone. For rare events, never say "% accurate": if floods happen on 1 day in 100, a model that always says "no flood" is 99% accurate and useless.
A company says "our flood model is 95% accurate". What two questions do you ask?
① Tested how? On ground the model never saw (block split), or on random squares with twins in training? ② Accurate at what? Floods are rare, so a model that always says "no flood" is right on most days and catches nothing. Ask how many real floods it caught, how often its warnings came true, and whether its chances are calibrated.

9Traps we actually fell into

Real stories from this project. Scientists respect a team that knows its own traps. Tell these.

Every game has bugs. These are six we found in our own work, and what each one taught us. Telling them makes scientists trust you more.

1 · Higher score, wrong mapAdding land cover lifted the flood WHERE score to 0.94. Its top squares were glacial lakes 360 m above any river. 97% of training rows were in the lowlands, so no test ever asked about a mountain (that's "out of distribution").👾 The ice-lake Glitch. The exam only had lowland questions, so it never noticed the Guardian thought mountain ice lakes were floodplains.
a good test score is not proof. Add checks the tests can't fake. Prefer causes (shape) over place-names (land cover)
2 · The official flood map was wrong70.5% of its "flooded" pixels sat on slopes steeper than 15°. Water doesn't pond on a cliff. It scored 0.53 against our layer, which is chance.👾 Water on cliffs. Water can't sit on a cliff, yet most of this official map's "flood" was on steep slopes. Even a Guild's chest can hold Glitches.
check a source before trusting it, even an official one
3 · A fake 0.87993% of squares scored exactly 0. Breaking ties by sort order gave AUC 0.879. Averaging ties properly gave about 0.5: chance.👾 The tie trick. When almost every block scores 0, the order you list them in can fake a good score. Fair tie-breaking showed it was a coin toss.
any AUC on mostly-zero scores needs a tie check
4 · The flood screen that passed everythingAn early radar check "confirmed" flood reports, but it also passed 97–100% of known dry dates. A new pre-registered radar test plus gauges gave 2,185 verified floods.👾 The detector that always beeps. A metal detector that beeps on plain sand finds nothing. Test your test on things you know are "no".
test the test on known "no" cases too
5 · The textbook rule that didn't holdTextbooks say picking "no-slide" squares carelessly inflates scores. Measured here: 0.860 vs 0.862, no difference, because Arunachal has almost no flat land.👾 The rule that didn't apply. The textbook warning is about flat land. Arunachal has almost none, so here it made no difference.
justify choices on what you measured, never on what textbooks say
6 · The fitted model that lostA fitted trigger (0.755) lost to a plain unfitted average (0.768). Only 72 dated slides is too few to fit on.👾 Fancy lost to simple. 72 practice questions are too few for a fancy alarm. The plain one won.
with little data, simple and fixed beats clever and fitted
🎮 hunting Glitches

Every game has bugs. A good Builder hunts them, fixes them and writes down what they learned. When you tell a scientist about a Glitch you caught yourself, they trust you more, not less: it shows you check your own work.

Your new model's test score went up. Why might you still throw it away?
Because a score only checks what the exam asked. The flood map with land cover scored 0.94, but it called glacial lakes 360 m above any river the most flood-prone places. The exam was nearly all lowland blocks, so it never asked about mountains. Look at the map, and check it makes physical sense.
