2Why slopes fail

A slope is a tug of war. Gravity pulls the soil downhill. Grip (friction plus roots and cement) holds it. When pull beats grip, it slides.

⛰️ Steepness
💧 Water in soil
🌿 Roots (trees)
grip ÷ pull (stays if above 1)

A toy version of the textbook "infinite slope" sum, to build intuition. It is not our model. Our model learns from 35,744 real landslides.

💧 Water is the key. Water fills the gaps between soil grains and pushes them apart. That cuts friction, so grip drops. That is why rain triggers slides.
🪨 Rock matters. Near a fault line, rock is shattered and weak. Some rock types weather into slippery clay.
🛣️ People matter. Cutting a road into a slope removes its foot. Many mapped slides sit along roads.

How people map "where slides can happen" (susceptibility)

Expert weightsa geologist scores each factor by hand
Statisticshow often slides sit on each kind of ground (frequency ratio, logistic regression)
Machine learning ✓ usgradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) learn from past slides
Physicswork out grip ÷ pull for every square. Needs soil depth and strength we don't have
Deep learningneural nets. Shine with images or millions of labels
🌳 Why trees, not deep learning? We have 35,744 mapped slides and a table of ~20 facts per square. On tables that size, trees win, train in minutes, and can explain each score, which a government officer needs. What we lack is not slides but dates of slides. Deep learning doesn't fix that.

3Rain as the trigger

The classic way to forecast landslides from rain is a threshold line. We tried it four ways, measured why it fails here, and replaced it with a sliding score. This section is the full story, with every number re-checked against the data.

The textbook idea (Caine 1980, Guzzetti 2008): plot past landslides by how long it rained and how hard. Draw the lowest line under them. Rain above the line = "slides can start".

I = a · D−b

I = how hard (mm per hour), D = how long (hours). The minus sign is the whole idea: long storms trigger at lighter rain, short ones need a downpour.

Two published lines are in our code (rainfall_features.py): Caine 1980 I = 14.82·D−0.39 and Guzzetti 2008 (global) I = 2.20·D−0.44.

What happened when we tried it here: four attempts

attempt 1 · failedGuzzetti's line on rolling windows
Checked against the last 1, 3, 7… 30 days of rain. Correlation 0.97 with plain 30-day rain: it was just a 30-day total in disguise. In a monsoon it rains nearly every day, so "the last 30 days" isn't a storm.
attempt 2 · works, but too looseThe same line on real storms
A "storm" = a run of days each with more than 1 mm. Now it carries its own information. But it is a global line and Arunachal is ~2.4× wetter than the world average: it fires on ~30% of days, about 116 days a year at a typical place. (Re-checked on the state's own rain squares. The doc's 24% was measured over the whole rain box.)
attempt 3 · came out backwardsFit our own line on our slides
The exponent came out +0.64, not negative. Here, longer "storms" are harder, not lighter (correlation +0.59). A wet run can last ~200 days: that's the monsoon itself, not a storm. A 1–2 day run is a passing shower of a few mm.
attempt 4 · right shape, can't be an alarmSwitch to "total rain vs duration"
E = 0.0115 · D1.644
E = total storm rain (mm), D = hours. Fits our data well (correlation 0.91) and sits under 89% of rainy-day slides. But it fires on 87–165 days a year, depending on the place.
See it with the real data every storm plotted by length and total rain · log scales

● 66 dated landslides that fell during a wet run (6 more fell on dry days: soaked ground giving way later, or no rain trigger at all).

● 400 ordinary rainy monsoon days, picked at random at the same places, with no recorded slide.

━ our fitted line, drawn so 90% of slides sit above it.

👀 Look at the grey dots: 74% of ordinary rainy days are above the line too. In a monsoon, most wet days already pass the lowest rain that has ever caused a slide. A yes/no line here is a weather report, not a warning.

💡 This isn't a coding mistake, and it isn't unique to us. The docs note the literature reports the same problem for monsoon climates. The textbook line was built for climates with distinct storms. Arunachal averages ~1,900 mm a year; much of the state gets 2,000–4,000.

Our trigger: "how unusual is this rain here?"

150 mm in 3 days is normal in the wet Siang valley and extreme on the dry northern crest. So each place is compared with its own rain history, not with one number for the whole state.

Made-up rain histories, just to show the idea. The real trigger averages the 3-day and 7-day percentiles against each point's own monsoon history.

📏 Percentile = "wetter than X% of days on record here". It is not a chance of a landslide.
🧮 Not fitted on purpose. With only 72 dated slides, a fitted model scored 0.755. The plain average scored 0.768. Fitting added noise.
🌱 Rain from earlier days ("antecedent rain") matters in theory. Tested: soil wetness and 15/30-day rain, on 72 and 583 events, even with fitted weights: no gain. The top metre of soil sits at 89–95% full from April to September, so "how wet already" barely varies.

The scoreboard: everything we tried, on the same test

The test: 72 dated slides (NASA's catalogue, 2008–2017) against 20,000 ordinary monsoon place-days picked at random from the same places. Score = AUC (pick one slide day and one ordinary day; how often does the score rank the slide day higher?). 0.5 = coin toss. All re-checked on 2 Nov 2026 from the raw rain data; every number in the docs reproduced.

⚖️ Read it honestly: with only 72 events, the shipped score's range is about 0.70–0.82. So the top few rows are statistically tied. We didn't pick 3+7 days because it clearly wins. We picked it because it's at the top, has nothing fitted, is easy to explain, and the 7-day half captures the multi-day soaking a geologist expects. The clear losers are the threshold-line versions and the fitted model.

Across the Himalaya: does the rule hold elsewhere?
The same rule on 583 dated slides along the Himalayan arc, with a different rain source (ERA5-Land): 0.69. Lower than Arunachal's 0.77 (different rain data, rougher locations), but clearly above chance on 8× more events. Adding soil wetness or 15/30-day rain there: no gain (at most +0.008).
Why "compare with its own history"? Measured.
One statewide line (3-day rain ≥ 80 mm, the state's own 90th percentile) gives:
Comparing each place with its own history gives every place about 18. The AUC test above can't show this (raw mm 0.754 vs own-history 0.761), because it only looks at places where slides were recorded. Fairness across the state is the real reason.
🔎 Found while checking: the docs call the threshold line "a floor", but nothing uses it as one. The line is fitted and saved (models/trigger.json) by trigger.py, but no code in the app or the daily cloud run reads it. Today it is explanation only: the physics a geologist expects, and the yardstick the sliding score was chosen over. If asked, say exactly that: "we kept the classic line for explanation; it fires too often here to be an alarm, so the forecast uses the sliding score."

The trigger, step by step, as it runs every day

1Fetch rain for the last days + the next 7 at 219 points (Open-Meteo).
2Add up 3-day and 7-day totals for each day.
3Look each total up in that point's own May–October history since 2010 (stored as 101 cut-points, so the app's file is tiny).
4Average the two percentiles → rain score 0–1.
5Multiply by the ground score (Phase 3) → the level on the map.

Designed and scored on NASA's 11 km satellite rain (2000–2026). The live app uses Open-Meteo's history, so the backtest re-scored the live version separately. Code: scripts/model/trigger.py, webapp/core/rainfall.py.

4Water on a height map

From a grid of ground heights alone, you can work out where every drop of rain goes. Step through it. The numbers are a tiny made-up valley.

🌊 HAND = Height Above Nearest Drainage (Nobre et al. 2011). A square 2 m above its stream floods long before one 40 m above it. It is the single strongest clue to where floods reach.
🗺️ How FloodSense uses it: its WHERE half reads only shape: height above the river, river size and how closed-in the valley is. Adding land cover made test scores higher but the map wrong (see trap 1 below).

5River forecasting

A gauge measures how high the river is. Officials draw lines on that scale. A flood forecast asks: will the river reach the line, and when?

🏛️ CWC (Central Water Commission) runs the main gauges and sets danger levels. Where none exists, our line is the river's own wettest 2% of days.
⏱️ Lead time = how far ahead. Big slow rivers give days of warning; small hill streams rise in hours. That's physics, not budget.
🎲 Our river forecast gives a chance the river reaches its line in the next 7 days, from rain plus the river's own recent week. A dotted level line goes 2 days ahead.

How Google Flood Hub does it: two models

🌍 Global river model
Forecasts river flow 7 days ahead from weather and catchment facts. Learned from ~16,000 gauges worldwide. Does not read the river's latest reading. Covers all the hill points in Arunachal.
📏 Level model (India and Bangladesh)
Reads the gauge's last week plus upstream gauges. Forecasts level 8–48 h ahead, with about 0.1 m error at 24 h. Works best on big, slow rivers, not flash floods.
🧭 Our stance: we build our own models on Arunachal's rivers, so we can prove them to clients. Google's model can be a cross-check on the big rivers, not our feed. Its code has been open since June 2026.

6Soil loss: the five-factor recipe (RUSLE)

The standard soil-conservation sum (Renard et al. 1997). Five numbers multiplied together give tonnes of soil lost per hectare per year.

Values are round, illustrative numbers for learning, not SoilSense outputs.

✖️ Multiplied, not added. If any one factor is near zero (flat ground, thick forest), the loss is near zero. Same logic as WHERE × WHEN.
🧱 "If untreated". P (bunds, terraces) is unknown until the department shares its treated-area records, so we set P = 1. Every SoilSense number means "if nothing has been done here".
⚠️ Trap we caught: India's common rain formula (Singh 1981) read 91% too low at Guwahati against a real gauge. We use a satellite rain-strength map (InRESatE) instead.

8Is the model any good? Four ideas

This is where reviewers attack. Learn these four, and the traps in section 9.

A · AUC: "pick a pair"

Take one random square that had a slide and one that didn't. Did the model score the slide square higher? AUC = how often it does. 0.5 = coin toss. 1.0 = perfect.

running AUC
–
SlideSense WHERE: 0.859 on unseen ground ≈ "6 times in 7"

B · Calibration: "does 20% mean 20%?"

A model can rank well (good AUC) and still say the wrong size of chance. Calibrated means: of all the days it said 20%, about 20% came true.

✅ FloodSense's river chances are calibrated: at a gauged river, 20% means about 20%.

⚠️ SlideSense levels are not chances. They are ranks, with a measured "× the usual".

Base rate: how common the event is anyway. If floods happen on 1% of days, "5%" is already 5× the usual.

C · Test on ground it has never seen (spatial cross-validation)

random split ✗
block split ✓

■ train   ■ test. Neighbouring squares are nearly identical. In a random split, every test square has a twin in training, so the score is too good (often 0.90+ in papers).

We hold out whole blocks of the state, 5 times over. Score: 0.859 ± 0.016. Honest, but lower than random-split papers. Not comparable: always ask which split a paper used.

Leakage = any way the answer sneaks into training. A random split is the most common kind.

D · A percentile max looks like 99% on a normal day

Take 97 rain points. Each one's percentile is a fair random draw on an ordinary day. What is the highest of the 97?

The old app showed this max as "0.99" for every town, every day. It looked like "99% chance of a landslide". It was just arithmetic. Now: show each place's own value, or the share of land at High.

9Traps we actually fell into

Real stories from this project. Scientists respect a team that knows its own traps. Tell these.

1 · Higher score, wrong mapAdding land cover lifted the flood WHERE score to 0.94. Its top squares were glacial lakes 360 m above any river. 97% of training rows were in the lowlands, so no test ever asked about a mountain (that's "out of distribution").
a good test score is not proof. Add checks the tests can't fake. Prefer causes (shape) over place-names (land cover)
2 · The official flood map was wrong70.5% of its "flooded" pixels sat on slopes steeper than 15°. Water doesn't pond on a cliff. It scored 0.53 against our layer, which is chance.
check a source before trusting it, even an official one
3 · A fake 0.87993% of squares scored exactly 0. Breaking ties by sort order gave AUC 0.879. Averaging ties properly gave about 0.5: chance.
any AUC on mostly-zero scores needs a tie check
4 · The flood screen that passed everythingAn early radar check "confirmed" flood reports, but it also passed 97–100% of known dry dates. A new pre-registered radar test plus gauges gave 2,185 verified floods.
test the test on known "no" cases too
5 · The textbook rule that didn't holdTextbooks say picking "no-slide" squares carelessly inflates scores. Measured here: 0.860 vs 0.862, no difference, because Arunachal has almost no flat land.
justify choices on what you measured, never on what textbooks say
6 · The fitted model that lostA fitted trigger (0.755) lost to a plain unfitted average (0.768). Only 72 dated slides is too few to fit on.
with little data, simple and fixed beats clever and fitted
