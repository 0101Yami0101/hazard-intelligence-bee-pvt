Level 3 · the training gym

Quick recap. A forecast is 🧨 TNT × ⚡ spark (Level 0). Blocks fall when pull beats grip, and the spark is rain that's unusual for that block (Level 1). Every block carries a 🎒 backpack of 34 facts (Level 2).

The big problem of this level: SlideSense has a huge pile of answers to where slides happen, but only a handful to when. Almost every choice in this level comes from that gap.

Level 3 in seven sentences: learn these first

  3. Only about 90 slides have a known day: the 📅 missing calendar pages. So the spark uses a simple fixed rule, not a trained brain.
  4. It sat a 🏋️ surprise exam on regions it never saw: 0.86, right about 6 times in 7.

🎮 the crater collection

Four Guilds' maps of old slides were merged into one big crater collection: GSI's outlines plus three Bhuvan surveys (2014, 2017, 2023). Two more lists (GSI's points and NASA's points) were kept locked away, only for checking later.

3Building the training set

A model learns from "yes" squares and "no" squares. Picking the "no" squares is the hard part. These are the rules, frozen with a fingerprint (b58e7dcc22134c1f).

To learn, the Guardian needs yes examples (crater blocks) and no examples (quiet blocks). Picking the quiet blocks carefully is the hard part.

  1. Only ground where slides can happen: steeper than 10°, not ice, water or moss. That's 85.7% of the state. Applied to yes and no alike, or the model learns nonsense.
  2. "No" squares at least 500 m from any mapped slide, so we don't pick unrecorded slides as "no".
  3. 5 "no" for every "yes", the same in every test block.
Three picking rules. ① Only ground where a slide could happen: no ice, lakes or flat fields. ② Quiet blocks at least 500 m from any known crater. ③ Five quiet blocks for every crater block.
📏 Textbook vs measured. Textbooks say careless "no" picking inflates scores. Here it made no difference: 0.860 vs 0.862. Arunachal has almost no flat land to cheat with (median slope 29°). We keep the rules anyway, for the reasons AUC can't see: 15.8% of a careless "no" pool sits within 500 m of a known slide.The rule that didn't change the score. 🎮 picking the quiet blocks

Imagine teaching someone to spot cracked eggs. You show them cracked eggs (yes) and good eggs (no). But if some of your "good" eggs are secretly cracked, they learn the wrong thing. That's why quiet blocks are picked at least 500 m from any known crater: a block right next to a crater might hold a slide nobody mapped.

Negatives: inside the domain (slope > 10°, not ice, water or moss; 85.7% of the state), at least 500 m from any mapped slide, at 1:5, balanced per fold, frozen with a fingerprint. Measured: careful vs careless sampling gave 0.860 vs 0.862.
Why must quiet blocks be at least 500 m from a known crater?
Because a block close to a crater could hold a slide nobody mapped. In a careless pool, 15.8% of the "no" blocks sat within 500 m of a known slide: probably some of them are unrecorded slides. Teaching with those is teaching with secretly cracked eggs.

4The WHERE model and how it was tested

LightGBM gradient-boosted trees, trained 5 times, each time hiding one block of the state.

SlideSense's brain is lots of little 20-Questions games added together. It was tested 5 times, each time on a region of the map it had never seen. It picks the right block about 6 times in 7.

Score on each hidden block (AUC, 0.5 = coin toss)

Two sets of models: the 5 "fold" models give every reported number. A 6th model trained on everything runs in the app, but may never quote a score: it has no hidden ground left. Quoting that one's score is how projects claim 0.99.

🏋️ Five surprise exams. Each time one region was hidden, the Guardian trained on the rest, then sat the exam on the hidden region. The 6th Guardian, trained on everything, works in the app, but it may never quote a score: it has already seen every answer.
How the map concentrates slides (share of land vs share of mapped slides)

10% of the state holds 71% of the mapped slides. That's the operational claim: survey a tenth of the land and cover seven in ten failures.

Search a tenth, find seven in ten. Careful, two different "× the usual": on the TNT map alone, High ground holds 4.4× its share of craters. The forecast levels on screen (TNT × spark, places and days) were measured separately: High is about 3×.
What it leans on (average SHAP, how much each fact moved the score)

Why can't the app's Guardian (trained on everything) quote its own score?

Someone says "your model just detects roads". How do you answer?

You met it in Phase 1. Here's what's under it.

The spark score asks one question: is the last 3 and 7 days of rain unusual for this block? It's a fixed rule, because only about 90 slides have a known day.

🎮 making a level

Every block, every day, SlideSense does one sum: TNT score × spark score. Then it drops the answer into one of five boxes, from Very Low to Very High. Each box carries a real, measured number: places and days in the High box have seen about 3× the usual number of slides.

Firm slope (0.10), record rain (0.99). What level, and why not Very High?

8The backtest: what would it have said?

AUC says it can rank. A disaster manager asks three different things: how often would it alert, what share of known slides would it catch, and does the warning come before the slope fails?

Replay mode: we replayed old monsoons and asked what SlideSense would have said. Its spark score was already raised 5 days before slides. And catching more slides always means ringing the alarm more often.

9The dead ends, and the doors still open

Every closed door below was tested, not guessed. Experts trust a team that knows what doesn't work. Learn each one with its single killer number.

We tried every door we could think of to find more slide dates. Most were locked, and we know exactly why. One is parked, and two are open.

closed · route 1Bhuvan's year as the date
11,329 slides are dated to a monsoon season (APSAC's SILAAS confirms it). But does a wetter monsoon carry more slides in the same place? 24–34% vs 33% chance: no signal. Survey effort drowns it.
closed · route 2Train a model on the 72 events
Fitted: 0.755. Plain average: 0.768. Too few events to fit.
closed · route 3Date slides from optical satellites
The best date window was 55–80 days, against the ≤7 days needed. 86% of slides happen under monsoon cloud.
closed · route 4Date slides from radar
The radar can see 99.4% of slide squares, but scars looked like any other hillside: 0.53 vs 0.50 chance.
closed · the circleValidate a dating method
Testing it needs slides that are both precisely placed and dated. GSI/Bhuvan have place, no date; NASA has date, rough place. The shortage blocks its own fix.
closed · sizeForecast how big
Which survey drew the outline explains 16.7% of slide size; all ground and soil facts explain 0%. Size measures the mapping team, not the hill.
smaller than hopedGSI's Landslidedata_1 table
The right fields (date, time, rain), but only ~402 rows for all of India. Maybe 0–20 for Arunachal. Useful, not the fix.
parked, not refutedPool the Himalayan arc
812 dated, located slides along the Himalaya (~10× ours), plus ~200–280 from GSI's reports. Train on the arc, test on Arunachal's own events. Beat 0.768 and it's proven. About a day of work to resume.
open · foundGSI's written field reports
48 Arunachal reports are openly downloadable. Since ~2016 they come as structured datasheets with exact date, time and location: ~25–40 new Arunachal events.
open · the askRoad-block logs (PWD, BRO, PMGSY)
Dated, located slides at scale. The best realistic way to grade the landslide forecast, which is written down daily since 10 Oct but not graded.
The doors, in game words. 🔒 Closed: season dates (too blurry), a trained spark brain (too few pages), dating from camera pictures (☁️ Cloud Curtain) or radar (scars look like any hillside), the circle (to test a dating trick you need dated slides), and slide size (it measures the survey team). ⏸️ Parked: borrowing the whole Himalaya's dated slides. 🔓 Open: GSI's field reports and road-block logs.
🎮 the locked doors

Every locked door here was tried, not guessed. That's what makes a Builder trustworthy: you can say exactly why each door is locked, with one number. The saddest one is the circle: to prove a trick for finding slide dates works, you need slides that are already dated and precisely placed, and those are the very thing we're short of.

Closed: Bhuvan's season years (24–34% vs 33% chance), a fitted trigger (0.755 vs 0.768), optical dating (55–80 day windows; 86% under cloud), radar dating (0.53), the validation circle, size (survey explains 16.7%, terrain 0%), GSI Landslidedata_1 (~402 rows nationally). Parked: Himalayan pooling (812 dated slides). Open: GSI field reports (~25–40 events), road-block logs.
Why can't you just date slides from satellite pictures?
Camera satellites can't see through the monsoon cloud: 86% of slides happen under it, and the best window we could get was 55–80 days, against the 7 needed. Radar sees through cloud, but slide scars looked like any other hillside (0.53, a coin toss).
What's "the circle"?
To test a method for dating slides, you need slides that are both precisely placed and dated. GSI and Bhuvan have the place but no date; NASA has the date but a rough place. So the shortage blocks its own fix.
