Guardian 2, FloodSense, watches the water. It's the deepest Guardian, with the most moving parts, so this level goes slowly. Learn the seven sentences first and the rest fits around them.

Level 4 · the river's height stick

Quick recap. Water rolls to the lowest block, and blocks only a little above a river flood first: ⬆️ steps above the river, or HAND (Level 1). Each river has a 📏 height stick with a 🟥 red line painted on it (Level 1). And only some numbers are 🎲 honest dice (Level 1).

🎮 three answers, three badges

A villager asks: "Will it flood?" FloodSense has three ways to answer, and each is a different question:

  • ✔ At a height stick: "Will this river reach its red line on Thursday?" A chance you can trust.
  • ≈ Anywhere: "Will there be a flood somewhere near you this week?" A rough guess: good at ranking places, rough on the exact size.
  • ◆ For one block: "If a flood comes, will water reach this spot?" Read from the ground's shape, and the same every day.
Six badges on screen: Forecast ✔, Outlook ≈, Estimate ◆, Measured ●, Input ☂, Record ◷. Never quote "spot X% today": spot × day is tiny, uncheckable, and runs ~4× high inside Arunachal.
Why never say "this spot has a 0.3% chance of flooding today"?
Because it mixes two answers (if a flood comes × will a flood come), the number is tiny, it can't be checked, and inside Arunachal it runs about 4× too high. Lead with the river chance instead.

🎮 the witnessed flood diary

Imagine a classroom where anyone can shout "there was a flood!". Some are right, some got the day wrong, some mean a river that just changed course. So FloodSense keeps a diary with one rule: a flood only goes in if a witness confirms it. There are three witnesses: 📡 Echo-Eye (the radar test), the 🌍 global flood archive, and a 📏 height stick that crossed its line.

The first radar witness was useless: it said "yes" to almost everything. The new one was tested on known dry days first, and passed. Fixing the diary was the biggest single jump in FloodSense's score.

2,180 verified floods: 1,153 by the validated radar test, 1,016 by the Global Flood Database, 125 by gauge crossings (routes overlap); 1,302 of 3,331 news reports confirmed, 878 floods not on the old list; 5 false gauge floods dropped by rule. The old screen passed 97% of known dry dates; the new test calls 97.8% of them "no".
Why isn't a news report of a flood good enough to train on?
Reports can have the wrong day or place, or describe a river shifting its channel (215 records were exactly that). The old radar check didn't help: it also "confirmed" 97% of dry days. Training on unchecked reports teaches a Guardian the wrong lessons.
If the radar witness says "no water", does that disprove the report?
No. A flood can drain away before the satellite passes over. A radar "no" just means that witness can't confirm it; the other two witnesses still can.

🎮 if a flood comes

This Guardian brain looks only at the shape of the ground: how many ⬆️ steps above the river a block sits, how big the river is, and whether the valley is an open plain or a narrow gorge. It learned from 2,806 satellite-confirmed floods. Then its numbers were calibrated, so they're the right size, not just the right order.

A new model scores better on the test. Why might you still reject it?
Because the test may not ask the right questions. The land-cover version scored 0.935, but its top blocks were glacial lakes 360 m above any river. 97% of training blocks were below 500 m, so the test never asked about mountains. Check that the map makes physical sense.

🎮 honest dice at the height stick

Here's what "honest" means. Suppose FloodSense said "about 15%" on 924 days. If the river really reached its line on about 15% of those days, the dice are honest. That's what happened: it said 15.9% on average, and the river reached its line 15.4% of the time.

It isn't perfect. When it said 50–75%, only 44% came true, on just 97 days. Knowing that one weak jar, and saying it, is what makes people trust the rest.

Calibration at 32 gauges, held-out years (1 day ahead): said 1.4% → 1.7%, 3.1% → 3.0%, 6.6% → 7.6%, 15.9% → 15.4%, 36.7% → 37.9%, 60.2% → 44.3% (n 97), 91.0% → 90.7%. Warning at 25%, 1 day ahead: 61% of crossings caught, 68% of warnings right; rain only: 10% caught. 7 gauges use CWC's official danger level; the rest their own wettest 2% of days.
A CWC engineer asks: "Your 20%: what does it actually mean?"
"On the days we say about 20%, the river reaches its line on about 20% of them. We checked that on years the model never saw: for example, it said 15.9% and 15.4% happened. The weak spot is 50–75%, where only 44% happened, on 97 days."
Is a river forecast "68% accurate"?
Don't say that. Say it both ways: warning at 25% one day ahead caught 61% of crossings, and 68% of its warnings were right. For rare events, "% accurate" hides everything: a model that always says "no" is right on almost every day.

7One wrong reading = a "52% flood"

On 28 Sep, CWC's feed held one reading of 2,231.30 m at Murga Bridge against a steady 2,134.30 m. Probably another station's value under the wrong code. The forecast read 52%.

One wrong number from one height stick (a jump of 97 m in one reading!) made FloodSense shout "52% flood!". Now every reading goes through a three-pass 👾 Glitch scanner first.

Why the old check missed it: it flagged a reading 3× its neighbours. On heights above sea level (2,134 m) that never fires.

Why the old scanner missed it: it looked for a reading 3 times bigger than its neighbours. On a river measured from sea level (2,134 m), a 97 m jump is only 5% bigger.

Now, three passes: (1) the old ratio test for zeros and slipped decimals; (2) impossible excursions: a level that leaves a steady stretch by more than 5 m and comes straight back within 72 h; (3) keying slips: a swapped digit (210.5 for 201.5).

On all 1.18 M readings: 194 removed, 18 false high-water days, all 18 confirmed false by four separate tests. The real landslide-dam surge at Tuting (Oct 2018) kept all 144 of its readings.

Catch the fakes, keep the real surprises. The scanner removed 194 of 1.18 million readings, but kept a real 2018 surge that looked wild.
🧬 What the false readings had done: Doimukh and Tamen had never really reached danger. And gauge-found "floods" resting on false readings were in the training list. Five are now dropped by rule. 🎮 the gauge Glitch

One morning, the Murga Bridge height stick sent one reading 97 m higher than all its neighbours. Rivers don't jump 97 m and come back. It was probably another station's number with the wrong label. But FloodSense believed it, and said 52%.

So now every reading goes through three scanners: ① a number far too big or small (zeros, slipped decimals), ② a jump that leaves a calm stretch by more than 5 m and comes straight back, ③ two digits swapped (210.5 instead of 201.5).

Murga Bridge, 28 Sep: 2,231.30 m against a steady 2,134.30 m → 52%. Three-pass filter on all 1.18 M readings: 194 removed, 18 false high-water days, all confirmed false by four tests; the Tuting 2018 surge kept. Five false gauge floods dropped from training; Arunachal timing rose 0.879 → 0.895.
Why did the old spike check miss a 97 m jump?
It flagged a reading 3× bigger than its neighbours. CWC levels are heights above sea level, so at 2,134 m a 97 m jump is only about 5% bigger: the ratio test never fired.

🎮 the river on the line

Most rivers are easy: far below the line, or rushing up to it. The hard case is a river sitting right on its red line, wobbling a few centimetres up and down. Tomorrow it might be 5 cm over (a "flood") or 5 cm under ("no flood"). FloodSense sometimes says "under 5%" on those days, and the river stays over.

Seven designs were tried, each with its pass mark written first. One (reading the river's own last week) helped a lot and is live. But none passed the strict test, so we stopped: no eighth try on the same old days.

9"Near here": the outlook for every place

Where there's no gauge, people still ask: "will it flood near me?" This is that number, and it's the approximate one.

Most places have no height stick nearby. For them, FloodSense gives a rough guess: the chance of a flood within about 10 km this week. It's good at which places, rough on how much.

How it's made
  1. Day model, per ~10 km rain area: how unusual today's rain is here and upstream × how much floodable ground the area has (from terrain, not from where floods were reported, so a valley nobody writes news about isn't told it never floods). Flat areas also read soil wetness.
  2. Combine the areas a 10 km circle touches, then the 7 days.
  3. Map to how often a recorded flood really fell within 10 km (calibration).
The area brain reads how strange the rain is (here and upstream) and how much low ground the area has. That comes from the ground's shape, not from where news reports were written, so a quiet valley isn't told it never floods.
