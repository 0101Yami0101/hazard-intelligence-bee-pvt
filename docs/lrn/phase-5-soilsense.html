2The recipe: five numbers multiplied

RUSLE, the standard soil-loss sum in Indian soil conservation (Phase 1 has the toy). Here is where each number comes from for Assam.

Rrain strengthInRESatE satellite map (0.1°)
Khow easily soil washesfrom SoilGrids, top 30 cm
LSslope steepness × lengthfrom the 30 m height map
Cground coverESA WorldCover class → value
Pprotectionunknown → 1 everywhere
A, soil lostt/ha/yrif untreated
🧱 "If untreated" is the honest label on every number. P is bunds and terraces. Nobody outside the department knows where they are, so P = 1 means "as if nothing has been done here". The department's treated-area records (an Ask) would fix it. Land the recipe doesn't apply to, such as rivers, water, towns and river land under 2 m above its channel, stays uncoloured, never "low". That's 30% of the state; 69.5% is scored.

3Which factor actually decides the answer?

How much each factor varies across scored land in Assam (1st to 99th percentile). A factor that barely varies can't change the ranking much.

Slope (LS) swings 640-fold; soil (K) only 1.2-fold. So the ranking is mostly slope × cover × rain. That's why the soil map's known weakness (it reads 0.57× the national ISED values) hardly touches the order of places, only the tonnes.

Main cause, across all 703 catchments

Each catchment's "main driver" = the factor most above the state average.

4The rain trap: a common formula read 91% low

R needs storm intensity, which most of India doesn't measure. People use a shortcut formula from yearly rain. It was checked against Assam's only gauge-derived value (Guwahati, R ≈ 7,924).

Chosen: InRESatE, a satellite-based rain-strength map (2001–2018). IRED is the second opinion, run alongside on every screen.

⚖️ The catch: the two good maps disagree on pattern (district ranking agreement only 0.26). So nobody can say yet which is right, and each top catchment says whether it stays on top under the other map. That's the most important honesty rule in SoilSense (section 8).

5The tree problem: one number moves everything

The satellite land map calls forest, tea gardens and jhum fallow all "tree". One cover value must stand for all three, and it's the top open question.

Share of scored land in each class slight <5 · moderate 5–10 · high 10–20 · very high 20–40 · severe 40–80 · very severe 80+ t/ha/yr
Main run (tree C = 0.03)
Same, but tree C = 0.003 (dense forest value)

Severe or worse: 17% of the land in the main run, 2% with the forest value. Average loss drops from 21.6 to 7.0 t/ha/yr. The map's colours on tree land are indicative, and the screen says so.

Why 0.03 for now: it's a class average across forest (low), tea (higher) and jhum fallow (much higher). The lower value runs alongside, one click away.
The fix tried: split "tree" into forest / tea / jhum with Sentinel-2. It told tea from forest well (0.85), but the blind picture check failed (0.20–0.44 against bars of 0.60–0.70). Village groves and undated imagery tripped it. Not adopted; a second try needs a new sample.
🌲 A second trap, the treetop guard. The height map measures treetops, not ground. On flat land, forest and tea-garden edges look like steep slopes. A guard caps those slopes on low land (below 20 m above its channel), on 36% of the state. Without it, "severe" would include lots of flat paddy edges.

6The map, and what it does not cover

✅ Covers: sheet and rill erosion: rain washing the top layer off sloping land. 100 m squares, all of Assam, six classes.
🚫 Doesn't cover: gullies, riverbank cutting or landslides. RUSLE doesn't model them. River land under 2 m above a big channel (22% of the state) is left unscored; the 5 m version is reported alongside.
⛰️ Hills ≫ plains, as it must be. A national 250 m map (IIT Delhi's) calls western Assam's plains the most erodible: its slope factor barely follows slope (2.7 on flat ground, 5.6 above 30°; ours 0.11 → 54). We disagree with it on purpose, and can show why.
🛰️ ISRO's land-degradation map was checked as a reference: it points the other way, and the gap sits on tree land (the same tree problem). It is also used, at arm's length, to train the AI second opinion (section 9).

7The ranked list: what an officer actually uses

703 small catchments and 35 districts, ranked. Each row says why and what helps.

Each catchment row carries:
  • its average loss and its rank
  • main cause (the factor most above the state average)
  • a second reason against similar slopes, e.g. "heavy rain, 2.5× their average"
  • typical measures: contour bunds, terraces, check dams, cover before the monsoon
  • how firm the rank is (next section)
  • what the lost soil is worth
Officer controls: sliders re-rank by the officer's own weights. A field kit exports 100 GPS points with form columns (CSV, KML, GPX, GeoPackage). The return path for filled forms isn't built yet.

What the soil is worth: farmland loses about 35 kg of nitrogen per hectare a year, about 1.7 bags of urea, roughly ₹61 crore a year statewide at the farmer's price. On tree land only a range is given (the tree problem again).

12The limits card

🧱If untreated: protection unknown. Ask: treated-area records.
⚖️Tonnes uncertain by a factor of several; ranks are the product. Paid / Ask: measured sediment, field data.
🌲Tree land is indicative: one value for forest, tea and jhum.
🌧️Rain map unsettled: two good maps disagree on pattern. Ask: intensity gauges.
🕳️No gullies, riverbanks or landslides: method limit; separate detectors needed.
☁️Clouds hide the monsoon ground: physics; dry-season composites instead.
🧪Not field-validated yet: the department's pilot is the test.
🛰️Earth Engine on a restricted, non-commercial quota: fix before commercial use.
