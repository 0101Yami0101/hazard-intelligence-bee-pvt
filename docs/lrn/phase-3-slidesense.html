⚖️ The whole story in one line: we have ~37,800 answers to WHERE and ~90 answers to WHEN. That is a gap of nearly 1,000×. So WHERE gets a trained model, WHEN gets a simple fixed rule, and most of the "dead ends" are attempts to close that gap.

2The labels: where slides have been mapped

Four maps of past landslides, made into one. Plus two point lists kept aside only for checking.

📐 Tiny slides. The median mapped slide is 944 m², about 30 × 30 m. 91.5% are smaller than one 100 m square. So a square counts as "slide" if an outline touches it at all. Otherwise 9 in 10 would vanish.
🧮 Three counts, all correct. 37,788 outlines from four maps. About 35,744 distinct slides once overlaps are removed. 91,610 squares touched (1.1% of the state).
🚫 No true "no". Nobody ever certified a square as slide-free. Most of Arunachal has never been walked. Absence of a record isn't absence of a slide.
🔑 Why "no true no" matters: the ground score can rank squares, but it can never be a probability. A probability needs the true count of slide-free places, and that doesn't exist. So a ground score of 0.5 does not mean a 50% chance.

3Building the training set

A model learns from "yes" squares and "no" squares. Picking the "no" squares is the hard part. These are the rules, frozen with a fingerprint (b58e7dcc22134c1f).

"yes" squares
90,799
"no" squares
453,995
  1. Only ground where slides can happen: steeper than 10°, not ice, water or moss. That's 85.7% of the state. Applied to yes and no alike, or the model learns nonsense.
  2. "No" squares at least 500 m from any mapped slide, so we don't pick unrecorded slides as "no".
  3. 5 "no" for every "yes", the same in every test block.
📏 Textbook vs measured. Textbooks say careless "no" picking inflates scores. Here it made no difference: 0.860 vs 0.862. Arunachal has almost no flat land to cheat with (median slope 29°). We keep the rules anyway, for the reasons AUC can't see: 15.8% of a careless "no" pool sits within 500 m of a known slide.

4The WHERE model and how it was tested

LightGBM gradient-boosted trees, trained 5 times, each time hiding one block of the state.

Score on each hidden block (AUC, 0.5 = coin toss)

Two sets of models: the 5 "fold" models give every reported number. A 6th model trained on everything runs in the app, but may never quote a score: it has no hidden ground left. Quoting that one's score is how projects claim 0.99.

How the map concentrates slides (share of land vs share of mapped slides)

10% of the state holds 71% of the mapped slides. That's the operational claim: survey a tenth of the land and cover seven in ten failures.

What it leans on (average SHAP, how much each fact moved the score)

Facing direction leads: north-facing Himalayan slopes hold moisture and snow differently. The steepest 25 m patch beats average steepness, which is why slope is worked out at 25 m.

"Your model just detects roads." Take facts out, re-test

No single suspect fact carries it. Ground shape + water alone (10 physical facts) keeps 97.7% of the score.

⚠️ Known weakness: high ground is under-warned

■ % of real slides ■ % of squares called High+. At 2,000–4,000 m the model under-calls. Fewer slides are mapped up there because it's hard to reach, and the model learned "high = safe". More model power won't fix it; better labels would.

6WHEN: the rain score

You met it in Phase 1. Here's what's under it.

the formula
average of two percentiles
How unusual the last 3 days and the last 7 days of rain are, each against that point's own monsoon history. Nothing is fitted, so nothing can overfit, and it's easy to explain to APSDMA.
the evidence
~90 dated slides
All from NASA's Global Landslide Catalog, 2008–2018, with rough locations. 72 line up with the 11 km rain history, and 84 with the live rain archive from 2010.
the score
0.768 vs 0.755
The plain average beat a fitted model on 6 rain facts. With ~72 events, fitting adds noise, not signal. Soil wetness and 15/30-day rain were also tested: no gain.
Measured: how much more common slides are, by how unusual the rain is (84 dated slides, 219 points, 2010–2026)

These "× the usual" figures were measured with rules written down before running (at least 8 slides per band, numbers must rise). The screen rounds them, because 84 slides can't support more precision.

7Make a level yourself

The exact sum the app runs: ground × rain, then four fixed cut-offs. Move the sliders.

⛰️ Ground score
🌧️ Rain score

Cut-offs: 0.02 · 0.06 · 0.15 · 0.30 (webapp/products/landslide/model.py). They are top-heavy on purpose: Very High is ~3% of the state, so the map doesn't paint a fifth of Arunachal red.

ground × rain
✖️ Why multiply, not add? A cliff in dry weather doesn't fail, and a downpour on flat ground does nothing. Multiplying keeps that: if either is near 0, the result is near 0. Adding would let a stormy day push flat ground into danger.

8The backtest: what would it have said?

AUC says it can rank. A disaster manager asks three different things: how often would it alert, what share of known slides would it catch, and does the warning come before the slope fails?

Does the warning come early? median rain score around the 84 dated slides (re-run 2 Nov 2026, 219 points)

Even 5 days before, the rain score at the failure site is already well above an ordinary monsoon day (0.73 vs 0.50), and it climbs to the event. Slides here follow wet spells that build over days, not one storm. Caveat: wet days cluster, so this shows risk is raised across a window. It isn't a 5-day pinpoint.

The trade-off: pick an alert line
alert when rain score ≥

There is no free lunch. Catching more means alerting more. Where to sit is a policy choice for APSDMA: the cost of a missed slide against the cost of an ignored warning. We suggest starting near 0.90.

🗺️ Alerts must be local. A single statewide alarm fires if anywhere is wet: ~100 days a year. Arunachal is 550 km wide and the monsoon lasts months. That's why the app maps levels instead of issuing one state number.
🚫 We never quote a false-alarm rate. An alert day with no recorded slide may be a false alarm, or a slide in an empty valley that nobody wrote down. With 84 dated slides in 16 years, nobody can measure it. Saying so is more credible than a made-up number.

9The dead ends, and the doors still open

Every closed door below was tested, not guessed. Experts trust a team that knows what doesn't work. Learn each one with its single killer number.

closed · route 1Bhuvan's year as the date
11,329 slides are dated to a monsoon season (APSAC's SILAAS confirms it). But does a wetter monsoon carry more slides in the same place? 24–34% vs 33% chance: no signal. Survey effort drowns it.
closed · route 2Train a model on the 72 events
Fitted: 0.755. Plain average: 0.768. Too few events to fit.
closed · route 3Date slides from optical satellites
The best date window was 55–80 days, against the ≤7 days needed. 86% of slides happen under monsoon cloud.
closed · route 4Date slides from radar
The radar can see 99.4% of slide squares, but scars looked like any other hillside: 0.53 vs 0.50 chance.
closed · the circleValidate a dating method
Testing it needs slides that are both precisely placed and dated. GSI/Bhuvan have place, no date; NASA has date, rough place. The shortage blocks its own fix.
closed · sizeForecast how big
Which survey drew the outline explains 16.7% of slide size; all ground and soil facts explain 0%. Size measures the mapping team, not the hill.
smaller than hopedGSI's Landslidedata_1 table
The right fields (date, time, rain), but only ~402 rows for all of India. Maybe 0–20 for Arunachal. Useful, not the fix.
parked, not refutedPool the Himalayan arc
812 dated, located slides along the Himalaya (~10× ours), plus ~200–280 from GSI's reports. Train on the arc, test on Arunachal's own events. Beat 0.768 and it's proven. About a day of work to resume.
open · foundGSI's written field reports
48 Arunachal reports are openly downloadable. Since ~2016 they come as structured datasheets with exact date, time and location: ~25–40 new Arunachal events.
open · the askRoad-block logs (PWD, BRO, PMGSY)
Dated, located slides at scale. The best realistic way to grade the landslide forecast, which is written down daily since 10 Oct but not graded.

10What reaches the screen

The maths didn't change when the screens were rebuilt on 10 Oct. How it's said did.

✖️ "3× the usual", not "4 of 5". The user rejected a scale position as a headline. Every level now carries its measured figure, with the 90% range behind an ⓘ.
📍 Valley towns snap uphill. 12% of the state's 4,648 settlements sit on flat, unscored ground. The search moves to the nearest scored slope and says so, with the distance. What threatens a valley town is the slope above it.
🛣️ Roads page. Kilometres of main road crossing High+ ground, by stretch.
📄 One-page report. A printable page with both forecasts for the place on screen.
📝 Written down daily since 10 Oct (records/landslide_forecast/), each day's rain at every point with fingerprints of the fixed files. Not graded: no dated slides flow in.
📏 Rain detail. The ground is 100 m sharp; the live rain is ~22 km. Say this when showing the map.

11The limits card

Say these before anyone asks. Each one is in the docs; none is a secret.

🎲Not a probability. Ranks and "× the usual" only. There are no true "no" places to build a chance from.
📅WHEN rests on ~84 dated slides. Every rain figure has a wide range. High's "3×" could be 1.4–4.4×.
🏔️Under-warns at 2,000–4,000 m, a label survey bias. Flag high corridors for manual review.
🌧️Rain is coarse: 11 km history, ~22 km live points. A budget limit, liftable with IMD gauges or a paid tier.
🚫No false-alarm rate, and we refuse to invent one.
📏No size or severity. Tested and closed.
⏳Not graded live yet. Logged daily, but grading waits for dated slides (road-block logs).
🌋Rain only. Earthquake-triggered slides aren't covered.
