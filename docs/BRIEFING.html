The Data Backbone

The idea, the logic, and what stands on it

As of 6 Oct 2026 The idea: 8 minutes Everything: about 17 Idea first, numbers last
Say this · 30 seconds

We are building the data backbone for mountain-hazard intelligence. The facts needed to see a landslide or a flood coming already exist, but they are scattered across satellites, government portals, river gauges and survey maps, in shapes that do not fit together. Every team that wants to use them spends months lining them up, and then that work is thrown away. We do it once. Every source is collected, checked, laid on one shared map of the state and kept current, with a record of where it came from. Anything can then be built on top. Our landslide forecast and our flood forecast are the first two products, and the second cost a fraction of the first. And because every forecast is written down and checked against what really happened, the backbone gets richer every season.

In one line

From fragmented mountain data to actionable intelligence. It is the pitch deck's own closing line.

The picture to hold

An app is a vehicle. The backbone is the road. We are building the road, and running the first two vehicles on it to prove it carries weight.

Why a backbone

The app is the easy part. Four things are broken underneath it.

They are the same four for every hazard, which is why fixing them once is worth more than any single product.

30 m · 250 m · 11 kmground height · soil · rain

The data exists, but in pieces.

It sits with NASA and the European satellites, GSI, ISRO, the state space centre, the water commission and others. Each has its own format and scale. Rock types come as outlines, rivers as lines. Some sit behind portals that look closed and are not.

Start. Publish. Stop.then the next team starts again

Every project starts from zero.

A landslide study spends months lining this up, publishes one map, and stops. The flood study next door does the same work again. Nothing is reused.

Jan = Augthe same map all year

A map made once goes stale.

The ground barely changes, but rain and rivers change every day. A one-off map cannot tell you about this week.

No scorecardsaid, but never checked

Nobody keeps score.

Forecasts are not written down and checked against what happened. So nothing learns, and nobody can prove a forecast was any good.

The result is what you see everywhere: static maps, one-off studies, pilots that never scale. Our own first forecast taught us this. The model was the small part. Finding, checking and lining up the data was most of the work, and all of it was reusable.

The idea

Do the hard part once, and let everything stand on it

It is a standing system, not a one-off study. This is the whole idea in one picture.

Six rules make it work

Rule 1

One map for everything

The state is cut into 8.2 million squares, each the size of a football field. Every source, whatever its shape, becomes one more column of facts about those squares.

WhyA model needs a table. And two products can never disagree about where a place is.
Rule 2

Keep the original, and write down where it came from

Raw files are never edited. Each carries its source, its terms of use, its link, the date it was fetched and a fingerprint.

WhyAnyone can audit a number. If a source goes offline we lose freshness, not the system.
Rule 3

Check a source before trusting it

Every new source is tested against something independent.

WhyOfficial data can be wrong. The only official flood map of the state put most of its "flooded" ground on steep slopes. One wrong river reading once looked like a 52% flood.
Rule 4

The backbone does not know which hazard it serves

Nothing in it says "landslide" or "flood". A product brings its own question.

WhyThis is what makes the second product cheap, and the third cheaper.
Rule 5

Two shelves

One holds ready-to-use data: the map, the facts, the rain history. The other holds answers: forecasts and risk layers.

WhyDifferent people need different shelves. A road planner may want the base layers. An officer wants the forecast.
Rule 6

Close the loop

Every forecast is written down before the day and graded after it. What really happened comes back in as new data.

WhyMore data makes a forecast fresher. Only knowing what happened makes it better.

How it works

Follow one file through

  1. STEP 1

    Collect

    A script fetches each source: satellite archives, government map servers, river gauges, and a live weather feed every day.

  2. STEP 2

    Keep

    The file is stored untouched, with its record.

  3. STEP 3

    Line up

    It is fitted to the shared map. An outline becomes "which rock am I on". A line becomes "how far to the nearest river, road or fault".

  4. STEP 4

    Describe

    Each square ends up with 34 facts, and each place with 26 years of daily rain.

  5. STEP 5

    Shelve

    That table is shelf one. It is the same for every product.

  6. STEP 6

    Ask

    A product asks its two questions of it: where can this happen, and is something setting it off now?

  7. STEP 7

    Answer

    The result goes on shelf two and onto the screen, place by place, for the next seven days.

  8. STEP 8

    Check

    The forecast is written down, and later graded against what happened.

What else can stand on it

More hazards, more places, more users

More hazards

Anything with the WHERE × WHEN shape fits.

  • Soil erosion, for Assam. Next. The department names five ingredients: rain, soil, slope, plant cover, land use. Four are already in the backbone.
  • Glacial-lake floods
  • Forest fire
  • Earthquake-triggered slides
  • Riverbank erosion, city flooding

More places

The satellite inputs are global, so the same steps run anywhere.

  • A new state needs a switch for the region, its own local records, and its own checking.
  • Assam is the first test. Its map is designed to line up square for square with Arunachal's, so a river crossing the border can be followed across it.
  • Then the rest of the Himalayan belt.

More users of the data itself

Through an open feed. None of this exists yet.

  • Road and infrastructure planners
  • Power and telecom operators with assets on unstable ground
  • Insurers and risk modellers
  • Researchers and agencies who need the lined-up base layers
  • The state's own systems, so a warning shows up where officers already work
  • Other startups building their own products

Why it gets stronger with time

It makes its own ground truth

The loop makes new evidence

Each forecast, once graded, becomes a dated, located example that did not exist before.

New kinds of data land on the same map

Our own sensors, drone surveys, field and citizen reports, old road-closure logs and news records. Each becomes one more column, or one more dated event.

Sensors go where the backbone points

It ranks the ground, so it tells you which slopes and rivers deserve an instrument. Their readings then come back in as data.

What is ours

Most inputs are public. What is ours is the assembly, the upkeep and the checked local history. Code can be copied. Seasons of checked local history cannot.

The two products, as proof

◭

SlopeSense

Landslide forecast
Live
  • Its WHERE questionHow weak is this ground? Learned from about 37,000 mapped landslides inside Arunachal.
  • Its WHEN questionIs the rain unusual for this exact place?
  • Where it standsA 7-day outlook for any place and for roads. WHERE picks the right spot about 6 times in 7. It gives a rank, not a percentage, and its forecasts are not logged yet.
◈

FloodSense

Flood forecast · the furthest along
Live
  • Its WHERE questionHow easily does water reach this spot?
  • Its WHEN questionRain here and upstream, plus the river's own level at 32 gauges.
  • Where it standsIt runs by itself every morning and grades itself against the real river. At a gauged river, when it says 20%, about 20% happen. It has not yet been through a live flood: logging began as the monsoon ended.

Honest limits

  • Wired to one state. Moving it to a second is designed, not built.
  • The loop runs for floods only, and there is no live catch yet.
  • No outside users yet. No open feed, no partner intake, no sensors.
  • It runs on one PC. Two days have been missed so far.
  • Sharing data outward depends on each source's terms of use.

What is next, in order

  1. Prove it moves. The Assam soil-erosion module: a new hazard and a new state at once.
  2. Close the loop for landslides and run the whole pipeline by itself, off the PC.
  3. Stamp every answer with its inputs, so any past forecast can be rebuilt.
  4. Open the feed. Only after the pipeline runs itself. A feed of stale data is worse than none.
  5. Let others write in, through a holding area, then add our own sensors.
  6. A pilot at Jang, Tawang, for ground truth in the mountains.

Questions you will get

Short answers to the likely ones

What exactly is the Data Backbone?

A standing system that collects every useful source, keeps the originals, lines everything up on one map of the state, keeps it fresh, hands it to products, and records what they said and what really happened.

Is it a product, or plumbing?

Today it is the engine under our two products. Later it is a product in its own right, through an open feed others build on.

Why not just build the landslide app?

Because the app is the last small part. The data underneath is most of the work, and it is the same data every hazard needs. Built once, it makes each next product cheap.

The data is public. What is yours?

The assembly, the upkeep and the checked history. Twenty-plus sources made to fit one map, kept current, with every forecast graded. Anyone can download the same files. Few will do the rest.

Who else would use it?

Road and infrastructure planners, power and telecom operators, insurers, researchers and agencies, the state's own systems, other startups. None yet. The open feed is planned.

How do you add a new hazard, or a new state?

A hazard brings its own two questions and its own record. Everything else is already there. A state needs a region switch, its own local records and its own checking. Assam is the first test.

What stops Google or ISRO doing this?

They could build the pipeline. The hard parts are local: getting these sources to fit, knowing which official layer is unusable, and seasons of forecasts checked against these rivers and slopes.

Is it live?

The backbone and two products are. The loop runs for floods. The open feed, partner intake and sensors are planned.

How good are the products?

Landslide WHERE picks the right spot 6 times in 7. At a gauged river, 20% means 20%. Never one headline number.

Has it caught a real event?

Not yet. It was tested hard on past years. The next monsoon is the proof.

Not in our docs: pricing and market size. The shape we do have is the ladder. A free statewide layer, then paid tiers.
