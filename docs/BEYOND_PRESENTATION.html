01Slides 1 and 13 · home page: the top and the close

The big idea

Every conversation starts here. If the first four are clear, the rest is detail.

1.1In one line, what do you do?★
Say

We build the data backbone for mountain hazards. Every useful source about the ground, the rain and the rivers is collected, checked and lined up on one map of the state, and kept current. Forecasts are built on top. Landslides and floods are the first two.

If they go further
So are you a data company or a forecasting company?
The backbone is what we build. The forecasts are how people use it, and how we show it works.
Careful

Do not open with “we are a landslide app”. Backbone first; the products are the proof.

1.2Is the backbone a product, or your internal plumbing?
Say

Today it is the engine under our two forecasts, and you can open it in the app: every source on record, every layer on one map. Later it becomes a product in itself, through an open feed that others can build on. That feed is planned, not live.

1.3Why not just build a good landslide app?★
Say

Because the app is the last small part. Most of the work sits underneath: finding the data, checking it, lining it up. And that work is the same for every hazard. Done once, the next product is cheap. Our flood forecast showed that.

The logic
  • Our own first forecast taught us this: the model was the small part, the data was most of the work, and all of it was reusable.
1.4Is this built, or is it a plan?★
Say

It is running. The backbone, the landslide forecast and the flood forecast are live, and you can open them now. The flood forecast runs by itself every morning and is checked every day. What is planned: the open feed for others, more hazards, more places, and our own sensors.

Careful

Anything drawn dashed is planned. Never speak of the open feed, sensors, alerts by SMS or another state as if it exists.

1.5Why Arunachal?
Say

Three reasons. The exposure: steep ground almost everywhere, a heavy monsoon, and long mountain roads where one closure cuts off a district. The gap: most work of this kind in India is in the western Himalaya, not here. And the importance: this is border terrain, where a blocked road matters to the whole country.

Only if asked

When we last checked, in mid-2026, the national landslide forecast bulletins covered neighbouring states and no district of Arunachal. Check it again before saying it in a room with people from that agency.

Careful

Never frame it as an agency having failed the state.

1.6What do the names mean: SlopeSense, SlideSense, FloodSense, Data Backbone?
Say

SlopeSense is the whole platform, the Hazard Intelligence Platform. SlideSense is its landslide forecast. FloodSense is its flood forecast. The Data Backbone is the shared data under both.

Careful

Older material, and the company website, may still call the landslide module SlopeSense. If someone notices: “We renamed it this month. The platform took the name.”

1.7Who would use this?
Say

First, the people who must decide before the event: district disaster management, roads and public works, water resources. Then anyone with assets on this ground: power and telecom lines, hydropower, transport operators. And later, through the feed, planners, insurers and researchers who want the data itself.

If they go further
Do you have users today?
Not outside our own team yet. That is exactly why we are in this room.
Careful

Do not claim users, partners or agreements.

02Slide 2 · home page part 1

The problem

The claim on the slide is that the facts exist but do not fit together. Expect people to test both halves.

2.1You say the data already exists. Where does it come from?
Say

From public bodies. Satellites give the height of the ground, the land cover and the rain. National and state agencies give the rock types, the soil, the mapped landslides, the rivers and the river gauges. Weather models give the rain for the week ahead. 29 sources today, and each one is on record in the app with where it came from and when.

Only if asked

The kinds of sources: American and European satellite programmes, the national geological survey, the national space agency’s portals, the state space centre, the central water agencies. The Data catalogue page in the app lists them.

Keep back

How each one is reached, and what had to be repaired in it. Some portals look closed and are not. Finding the way in took months, and it is part of what we own.

2.2If the data is all public, what is actually yours?★
Say

Three things. The assembly: 29 sources made to fit one map. The upkeep: it is fetched and checked every day, not once. And the checked history: every forecast written down and graded. Anyone can download the same files. Very few will do the rest. And nobody can download seasons of checked local history.

Careful

Do not say “we own unique data”. Most inputs are public. Ours is the assembly, the upkeep and the checked record.

2.3Why has nobody done this already? Not even the government?
Say

Pieces exist. There are hazard maps, global flood models, sensor companies, department portals. What does not exist is all of it joined for this terrain, running daily, and checked. Partly because it is a lot of unglamorous data work before anything shows on a screen. And partly because each office is responsible for its own piece. Joining them is nobody’s job.

Careful

Each agency does its own part well. Say that. The gap is the joining.

2.4There are already landslide maps. How is this different?★
Say

Those maps answer where. They look the same in January and in August. We add when: today’s rain and river, against that place’s own history, for the next seven days. And we keep score. We use the official maps as a cross-check, not as an input.

If they go further
Is your map better than the official one?
It is finer and newer, and the two agree on the broad picture. Ours also changes every day, which a printed map cannot.
Keep back

We have compared our ground map with the national one, number against number. Do not volunteer the result in a room: it sounds like an attack on the people we want records from.

2.5What do you mean by “nobody keeps score”?
Say

A forecast is made, the day passes, and nobody goes back to see whether it was right. So nothing improves, and nobody can prove it was any good. We write every forecast down before the day and check it after. For floods that happens every day now, against the real river readings.

If they go further
And for landslides?
Written down every day. Not graded yet. For that we need landslides with a date, such as road-closure registers. That is one of the things we are asking for.
2.6Do you have the right to use all this data?
Say

Almost all of it is published as open data, and each source’s terms of use are recorded with the file. At this stage we use it to build and to demonstrate. Before anything is sold or passed on, each source’s terms are checked again, and permission is asked where a source requires it.

Careful

Never say a licence, a permission or a partnership is secured.

2.7“Maps go stale.” How often does yours change?
Say

The ground layers change rarely, and we refresh them when a source publishes a new version. Rain and rivers are fetched every day, and the forecast is made fresh every morning.

03Slide 3 · home page part 2

The idea: one shared map

The squares are the picture people remember, so this is where the practical questions come.

3.1What exactly is the “shared map”?★
Say

Picture the state as a sheet of graph paper. Each square is 100 metres across, about a football field. There are 8.2 million of them. Every source, whatever shape it arrived in, is turned into one more fact about each square. So any square can be read straight down: how steep, what soil, what rock, how far from a river, how much rain.

The logic
  • A forecast needs a table: one row per place, one column per fact. The shared map is that table.
  • Two products can never disagree about where a place is, because they read the same squares.
3.2How do you fit a line, an outline or a single dot onto squares?
Say

Each shape becomes a question the square can answer. An outline of rock types becomes “which rock am I on”. A river or a road, which is a line, becomes “how far am I from the nearest one”. A broad reading, like rain, is shared by every square under it.

3.3Why 100 metres? Why not finer?
Say

It is a balance. Much finer, and there are close to a hundred million squares, so every test takes days and we try fewer ideas. Much coarser, and a cliff and a meadow are averaged into one gentle slope. 100 metres keeps the real slope visible and still lets us work fast.

If they go further
So you forecast for each 100-metre square?
No. We work on 100-metre squares. The answer is honest for an area: a town, a stretch of road. Not for one house. Showing it finer than the data supports would be false precision.
3.4Rain comes every 11 km. How can it say anything about a 100-metre square?
Say

It does not become finer. Every square under the same rain reading shares that reading. What differs from square to square is the ground. So the ground half is detailed, the rain half is broad, and the forecast is honest for an area. Ground rain gauges and weather radar are what make the rain half finer. That is the “ask” step.

The logic
  • We also do not judge rain in raw millimetres. We ask how unusual it is for that place, which holds up much better on broad rain data.
3.5How do you know the shape of ground nobody has surveyed?
Say

Satellites have measured it. There is a height map of the whole world made by radar in orbit, with a reading every 30 metres. From height alone you can work out how steep a slope is, which way it faces, and where water will run. That is why a small team can cover a whole state.

If they go further
Is 30 metres good enough?
For scanning a state, yes. For one particular slope you want finer: a drone or a ground survey. That belongs to the paid step, on chosen spots.
3.6Is this a GIS? Are you a GIS company?
Say

GIS is the craft of working with map data, and we use it everywhere: it is how the sources get lined up. But a GIS is a tool a person uses to look at maps. This is a system that keeps the maps current by itself and turns them into a forecast every morning.

The logic
  • GIS: geographic information system. DEM: digital elevation model, which is the height map.
3.7How many facts does each square hold? Which ones?
Say

A few dozen. The shape of the ground, the soil, the rock, the land cover, the drainage, and the distances to rivers, roads and fault lines. Plus the rain history for every place.

Keep back

The exact list, and how each fact is worked out.

3.8How big is all this?
Say

About 300 GB of original files, most of it satellite radar scenes. What the app itself needs is boiled down to a few megabytes. That is why it opens quickly and runs on modest hosting.

The logic
  • The heavy work is done beforehand and stored. Only today’s rain and rivers are fetched live.

04Slide 4 · home page part 3

How it works: the pipeline

Five steps and a way back. The questions here are about what happens on an ordinary morning, and on a bad one.

4.1Can you follow one file through for me?
Say

Take the height of the ground. It arrives as satellite tiles. We keep those untouched, with a note of where they came from. Then it is fitted to the squares and turned into steepness, the way each slope faces, how water drains, and how high each square sits above its river. That goes on the shelf. The landslide forecast reads the steepness. The flood forecast reads the height above the river. One file, two products.

4.2What does “keep the original” give you?
Say

Three things. Any number on any screen can be traced back to the file it came from. If we find a mistake in our own processing, we rebuild from the original instead of patching. And if a source goes offline, we lose freshness, not the system.

4.3What if a source stops, or a feed is down on the day?
Say

The system keeps running. We hold our own copy of everything historical. If today’s rain or a gauge feed is late, the forecast is less fresh, and the screen says so. It never fills a gap with a guess.

The logic
  • A real case to point at: the flood screen has a tile for how many gauges reported. On 11 October one whole feed was down at its source, and the screen showed 15 gauges offline.
4.4What are the “two shelves”?
Say

One shelf holds the ready-to-use data: the map and all its facts, the same for every product. The other holds the answers: the forecasts. Different people want different shelves. Someone planning a road wants the base data. An officer on duty wants the forecast.

4.5“Written down before, checked after.” How, exactly?★
Say

Each morning the forecast is saved to a log before the day it speaks about. When the day has passed, the real reading is fetched and set beside what we said. For rivers that is simple: the gauge tells us whether the river reached its line. The running score is on the track record panel in the app.

If they go further
Can you change a past forecast?
No. The log only grows. Even a wrong one stays. One morning a faulty river reading made us raise three false alarms. They are still in the record, on purpose.
How do you know what really happened with a landslide?
That is the hard one. There is no gauge for a landslide. We need dated reports: road-closure registers, district records, field reports. The forecasts are already being saved, so they can be graded the day those records arrive.
4.6Is it automatic? Where does it run?★
Say

The flood forecast runs by itself every morning. It fetches the rain and the rivers, makes the forecast, saves it, and grades the earlier ones. The landslide forecast is saved from the same run. Today that run is on our own machine. Moving it to a server, so it never depends on one computer, is on our “what comes next” list.

If they go further
Has it ever missed a day?
Yes, two mornings, when the machine was off. The log shows the gap. It does not hide it.
Careful

Do not say “always on” or “in the cloud”. The public link shows the latest forecast we have published. Today’s forecast is on our own machine.

4.7What do the three numbers mean: 29, 8.2 million, 26 years?
Say

29 is the number of separate sources lined up, each on record in the app. 8.2 million is the number of 100-metre squares inside the state. 26 years is the daily rain record, from 2000 to today, for every place. That record is what “unusual for this place” is measured against.

4.8Where do 26 years of rain come from, if the state has so few rain gauges?
Say

From satellites. Rain has been measured from orbit every day since 2000, for the whole state, with no gaps. It is broader than a gauge, about 11 km, but it covers every valley, including the ones with no instrument at all.

If they go further
Is satellite rain accurate?
Good at saying which days were wet and how unusual they were. Weaker on exact millimetres, most of all on high ridges. That is why we ask “how unusual”, not “how many millimetres”, and why ground gauge records are on our ask list.

05Slide 5 · home page part 3

The rules we keep in mind

Each rule invites the same follow-up: show me. Have one example ready for each.

5.1You said even official data can be wrong, and you caught it. Give me an example.★
Say

Two. One official flood layer for the state marked most of its flooded ground on steep slopes. Water does not stand on a hillside. So we could not use it to test anything, and we built our own verified flood record instead.

And one morning a public river feed carried a single wrong reading, about a hundred metres off. Our forecast jumped to 52%. We traced it that day and changed how readings are checked. The source corrected its value a few days later.

The logic
  • The point is that we check, not that anyone erred. Every new source is tested against something independent before it is trusted.
Careful

Do not name the agency in a room. Say “an official layer” and “a public feed”.

5.2How do you check a source?
Say

Against something independent. One example: from our own height map we work out where water should run, then lay that over the published river lines. If the two sit on each other, the position, the scale and the direction are all right, in one test.

Keep back

The full list of checks, and what each one caught.

5.3What does “test on ground it has never seen” mean?
Say

When we test a forecast, we hide whole regions while it learns, then ask it about those regions. Neighbouring squares look alike, so testing on the square next door is an exam with the answers showing. Our way gives lower scores, and honest ones.

5.4What does “label every number” mean?
Say

Every figure on screen carries a small tag saying what kind it is: a forecast we check every day, an approximate outlook, an estimate, a real measurement, an input such as rain, or a past record. So nobody mistakes a rough outlook for a measured fact.

5.5“Say so when unsure.” What does that look like?
Say

Three ways. Ground the landslide forecast was not built for, such as ice, open water and flat plains, is shown grey as “not scored”, never as low. The far end of the week is drawn dashed, because rain forecasts weaken that far ahead. And when a gauge is offline the screen says offline. It does not show an old number as if it were fresh.

Only if asked

About one seventh of the state is “not scored” for landslides.

5.6What does “no hazard built in” mean in practice?
Say

Nothing in the backbone mentions landslides or floods. It only knows the ground, the rain and the rivers. A product brings its own question. In our app, a new module is a new folder and one line in a list.

5.7Are there more rules than these?
Say

Yes. These are the main ones. The rest are about how we work: write the pass mark down before running a test, never adjust a forecast on the data used to judge it, and keep failed experiments on record so nobody repeats them.

Careful

Never say “six rules” or give a count. Say “rules we keep in mind”.

06Slides 6 and 7 · home page part 4

A product is a question; the same layers, reused

This is the logic of the whole pitch. “Two files” is the line people will push on hardest.

6.1Why split it into where and when?★
Say

Because they are two kinds of fact. Where is about the ground, which barely changes, so it can be worked out carefully, once. When is about rain and rivers, which change daily, so it must be fetched every day. Neither warns anyone alone. A weak slope in a dry week is quiet. Heavy rain on firm, flat ground does little.

6.2Why “times”? Why multiply the two?
Say

Because you need both at once. If either half is near zero, the answer should be near zero. Adding them would raise an alarm on a weak slope in a dry month.

6.3What does “rain that is unusual for that place” mean?
Say

Some parts of the state get several times the rain of others. 150 mm in three days is ordinary in the wet foothills and rare on a dry northern ridge. One rain limit for the whole state would alarm the wet parts all monsoon and never warn the dry ones. So for each place we compare today’s rain with that place’s own 26 years.

6.4Is every hazard really that shape? What about earthquakes?
Say

Anything with a place where it can happen and something that sets it off. Forest fire: fuel and terrain, times dryness and wind. Soil erosion: slope, soil and plant cover, times how hard the rain falls. Landslides set off by an earthquake fit too, with the shaking as the trigger. We do not forecast earthquakes themselves. Nobody can.

6.5Only two files for a whole flood forecast? Really?★
Say

For the first working flood module, yes. The map, the rain history, the live rain, the place search and the maps on screen were all reused, and it added two files of its own logic. Since then it has grown its own parts: the river gauges, a verified flood record, the daily check. And those went into the backbone, so the next product gets them without building them.

Careful

Say “the first flood module added two files”. Do not say “the flood forecast is two files”: it has grown, and a technical listener will know that.

6.6What did floods need that was not already there?
Say

Three things: the river gauges, a record of past floods we could trust, and the log that grades forecasts. All three are part of the backbone now, and the landslide forecast is written down the same way.

6.7How much faster was the second product than the first?
Say

The first took most of our time, and almost all of that went into data. The second reused it and reached a first working version in a fraction of the time.

Careful

We have no measured figure. Do not invent weeks or a percentage.

6.8What do the hollow dots for soil erosion mean?
Say

Planned. Soil erosion needs five ingredients: rain, soil, slope, plant cover and land use. Four of the five are already in the backbone.

Careful

Soil erosion is named as the next hazard. Do not name a place or a date for it.

07Slide 8 · home page part 4

Running today: the app and the demo

Once the app is open, the questions are about what is on the screen in front of them.

7.1Is this real data, or a mock-up?
Say

Real. The forecast on screen was made from today’s rain and river readings. Search any town in the state.

Careful

Stay inside Arunachal. Never press “Run now” in front of people: it takes one to three minutes.

7.2Why are all the numbers so low?★
Say

Because it is October. The monsoon has ended and the rivers are low. A forecast that stays quiet in a quiet month is doing its job. In the monsoon the same screen is far busier.

The logic
  • Say it before they ask. Grey means quiet; colour appears only when a chance is worth noticing.
7.3Can we open it ourselves?
Say

Yes. There is a public link, and it opens on a phone. It shows the latest forecast we have published.

Careful

The public copy is refreshed by hand, so it can be a day or more behind our own machine.

7.4What am I looking at on the Data Backbone screens?
Say

Three things. The pipeline: the whole system in one picture, solid where it is built and dashed where it is planned. The data catalogue: every source, where it came from and when it was fetched. And the layer explorer: any one layer of the shared map, for the whole state.

7.5Can it give me something to hand over?
Say

Yes. Each forecast page has a one-page report: a printable summary for the place on screen.

7.6Does it work on a phone? Without a connection? Does it send alerts?
Say

It works in a phone browser today. It needs a connection. It is in English. Alerts by message are planned, not built. What exists today for passing a forecast on is the printable one-page report.

Careful

Alerts, an offline mode and other languages are not built. Do not promise dates.

7.7Does it show risk to people, or only the hazard?
Say

Today it forecasts the hazard: where the ground or the river is dangerous. Roads are already laid over it, so you can see how many kilometres of main road cross ground at a high level. Settlements and population are in the backbone; joining them into the forecast is planned.

The logic
  • Hazard is the dangerous thing itself. Risk is the hazard plus what it would hit. An officer with limited resources needs the second.

08Slide 9 · home page: “More about SlideSense”

SlideSense: the landslide forecast

What it asks: weak ground, times rain that is unusual for that place. Keep to the logic; the recipe stays home.

8.1How does it know where the ground is weak?★
Say

It learned from the state’s own record. Tens of thousands of landslides have been mapped here by national agencies. We show the computer those places, and many places with no landslide, with the facts about each: steepness, the way the slope faces, soil, rock, cover, drainage. It finds the combinations that keep turning up where slopes failed. Then it scores every square in the state the same way.

Only if asked

About 37,000 mapped landslides, all inside Arunachal.

Keep back

Which facts matter most, how the no-landslide places are chosen, and the settings.

8.2How does it decide when?
Say

From rain. For each place it takes the rain of the last few days and of the week ahead, and asks how unusual that is for that exact place, against 26 years. Weak ground and unusually wet: the level goes up.

Only if asked

For a technical listener: the rain half is a clear rule, not a learned model. Very few landslides here have a recorded date, and when we tried to learn the timing from so few, it did worse than the rule. We will switch when the dated record justifies it.

Keep back

The exact days counted and how the two halves are joined.

8.3What does “High” mean? Is it a chance?★
Say

It is a rank, not a percentage. High means: look here first. Beside it we show a measured figure. At High, landslides have been about 3 times as common as usual. At Very High, about 10 times.

If they go further
Why not just give a percentage?
Because nobody has an honest one. The records say where slopes failed, not how many slopes held, and many landslides are never reported. A percentage would be invented. A rank with a measured “times the usual” is what the data can support.
Careful

The “3 times” and “10 times” rest on about 84 dated landslides. Always say “about”.

8.4How far ahead, and how exact in place?★
Say

Seven days, with the first days firmer than the last. In place: an area. A town, a stretch of road. Not a single slope, and not an hour.

If they go further
Will it ever tell me this slope fails tomorrow afternoon?
Not from satellites and open data. With instruments on that slope, yes, for most slopes: they creep before they fail, and the creep can be measured and projected forward. That is the paid step, on chosen slopes. One limit stays for everyone: sudden flows and rockfalls give no sign beforehand.
8.5Does Very Low mean safe?
Say

No. We never use the word safe. Very Low means landslides have been rare on ground like this, in rain like this. It does not mean they cannot happen.

If they go further
And the grey areas on the map?
Not scored. Flat land, water, ice, or outside the state. Grey is not “low”.
8.6What does it do for roads?
Say

There is a Roads page. It shows how many kilometres of main road cross ground at a high level today, where those stretches are, and the outlook along any route between two towns.

8.7What about landslides not caused by rain: earthquakes, road cutting, blasting?
Say

Today the trigger is rain, which sets off most landslides here. Road cutting shows up in part, because distance to a road is one of the facts about the ground. Earthquake shaking is planned as a second trigger, and its layer is already in the backbone. Construction and blasting records are on our ask list.

8.8How is it different from the weather department’s rain warning?
Say

They forecast rain. We forecast what that rain does to this ground. The same 100 mm matters on one slope and not on the next. A rain warning tells a whole district the same thing; this tells you which parts of it are loaded. We use weather forecasts as an input. We do not compete with them.

09Slide 10 · home page: “More about FloodSense”

FloodSense: the flood forecast

What it asks: ground water can reach, times rain and the river. It is the product furthest along, so it draws the most detailed questions.

9.1How does it know where water can reach?
Say

From the shape of the land. How high a spot sits above its river, how big that river is, and how closed-in the valley is. Low ground beside a big river floods. A ridge does not. That half was learned from past floods mapped by satellite, and it is the same every day.

Keep back

The exact inputs, and the flood record itself.

9.2And how does it decide when?★
Say

Two ways. Near any place: from the rain there, and the rain upstream that will arrive later. And on a river with a gauge: from the river’s own readings over the past week, plus the rain to come. That gives the chance the river reaches its danger line on each of the next seven days.

The logic
  • A slope fails from rain that lands on it. A river rises from rain that lands anywhere upstream. So floods need the catchment: all the land that drains to a place.
  • The timing was learned from verified floods: confirmed by satellite radar, a global flood database or a river gauge. Use those words.
9.3What is a river gauge, and whose are they?
Say

A station that measures a river’s level or flow many times a day. They belong to the central water agencies and report in public. We read 32 of them across the state, some with records going back decades.

Careful

We own no gauge. We read public feeds.

9.4What does 20% mean on a river?★
Say

The chance that the river reaches its danger line on that day. And it is meant literally: over past years, on the days it said about 20%, the river reached its line about one time in five.

If they go further
And the percentage shown for a town?
That one is an outlook: an approximate chance of a flood somewhere within about 10 km. It is tagged approximate on screen, because it cannot be checked as cleanly as a river against its own gauge.
What is “its line” where there is no official danger level?
Where the agency has set a danger level, we use that. Where it has not, we use the level that river reaches only on its wettest few days.
9.5What does the track record show today?★
Say

Since 21 September every river forecast has been written down: about 4,500 so far, and about 2,100 of them already checked against the real river. In that time no river reached its line, and the forecasts said so: almost all were under 2%. So far it proves the machinery works. The real examination is the next monsoon.

If they go further
It shows three warnings and none of them right?
Yes. All three are from one morning in September, when a public feed carried a wrong reading. We fixed the checking that day. We left the false alarms in the record on purpose: a record you can tidy is not a record.
Careful

These figures move every day. Read them off the panel on the day.

9.6What about rivers and streams with no gauge?
Say

They get the outlook from rain and terrain: the chance of a flood in that area. Without a gauge we cannot follow that river’s own level. A gauge, or a low-cost level sensor, changes that. Gauges that offices already run are the ask step. Our own sensors are the paid step.

9.7Flash floods and cloudbursts?
Say

Honestly, a cloudburst over one small valley is the hardest case. The rain forecast we read is too broad to see it coming, and it is over in hours. What we give is the wider picture: these valleys are primed this week. Warning by the hour needs weather radar, or a sensor upstream. With those it becomes possible.

9.8Dam releases? Glacial lakes?
Say

Not included today. A release from a dam is a decision, not weather. With the operators’ release schedules it can be added, and that is on our ask list. Glacial-lake floods are a separate hazard with their own data. It is on the longer list, not being built now.

Careful

Glacial-lake floods were taken off the slide. Do not promise them.

9.9Can it show how deep the water will be, and which houses?
Say

Not today. It says how likely water is to reach a spot if a flood comes. Depth needs the river’s cross-section and a detailed survey of the ground. With those, yes. That is the paid step, for chosen towns.

9.10How far ahead does it see a river?
Say

For each of the next seven days, the chance of reaching the line. And for the river’s actual level, a forecast line two days ahead at the larger gauged rivers, with a range around it. Beyond two days we show the chance only, because the level cannot be drawn honestly that far yet.

9.11How is this different from the Central Water Commission’s flood forecasts?
Say

We do not replace them. Their forecasts and warnings are the official ones, issued for chosen stations. We read the same public gauges and add three things: a chance for each of the next seven days at every gauge, an outlook for places away from any gauge, and a public score of how each forecast did.

Careful

Never say “better than” an official body.

9.12And from Google’s Flood Hub?
Say

Google’s is one model for the rivers of the whole world. For hill rivers here it works from weather alone, and does not read the river’s latest level. Ours is trained on these rivers and reads their gauges every morning. We respect their work. They have opened their model, and we plan to run it beside ours as a second opinion. Ours makes the call.

10Any slide · most often after 9 and 10

The AI inside

Someone will ask what kind of AI this is. One clear sentence beats a list of techniques.

10.1Is this AI? What kind?★
Say

Yes: machine learning. The computer is shown past events and the facts about each place, and finds the pattern itself. Nobody writes the rules by hand. The kind we use is built for tables of facts, not for pictures or text, and it can show which facts drove each answer.

Only if asked

For a technical listener: gradient-boosted decision trees. Many small sets of yes-or-no rules, each one correcting the last. It is the standard choice for tables.

Keep back

The inputs, the settings, how the examples are chosen, and how a raw score becomes an honest percentage.

10.2Why not deep learning?
Say

We use the tool the data justifies. Our data is a table of facts about places. On tables, this family of methods is still the most accurate, quick to retrain, and able to explain itself. Deep learning earns its place on images and long sequences, and it is in our plan for exactly those: reading radar images, and following a river hour by hour.

10.3Why can’t ChatGPT do this?
Say

A chat model learned from text. It has never measured a slope here, and it cannot tell whether last week’s rain was unusual for one valley. This is a measuring problem, not a language problem. Where a language model can help us is reading: pulling dated events out of old reports and news.

10.4Is it a black box? Can you explain one forecast?
Say

No. For any place the screen shows the two halves apart: how weak the ground is, and how unusual the rain is, with the rain in millimetres. An officer can see why the level is what it is. Behind that, the method can list which facts pushed a score up or down.

10.5How do you stop it fooling you?
Say

Three habits. Test only on regions and years it never saw. Write the pass mark down before running the test. And add a check a score cannot fake: look at the map. Once, a version scored better on paper and put its highest flood risk on lakes at the top of mountains. We threw it out.

10.6Does it learn by itself over time?
Say

Not by itself, and that is on purpose. The record grows every day without us. Retraining is a deliberate step, after a season or a real batch of new events. And the new version must beat the old one on the same test before it goes live.

10.7What comes next on the modelling side?
Say

Several methods answering the same question side by side, with a referee that learns which one to trust where. A method is added only if the whole panel does better with it. As the checked record grows, that includes deeper models.

Keep back

Which methods, and how the referee works.

10.8Whose technology is it? Do you use anyone else’s models?
Say

The models are ours, trained on this state’s own records. The inputs, such as satellite data and weather forecasts, are public. Where a good open model exists we may run it beside ours as a second opinion, and say so.

10.9The climate is changing. Won’t the past mislead it?
Say

It is a fair worry, and two things help. “Unusual” is measured against a long record that keeps growing, and we retrain on a schedule, not once. And heavier rain makes a daily forecast more useful, not less. A fixed map cannot follow it.

10.10What computing does it need?
Say

Little. The heavy work is done once and stored. The daily run takes a few minutes on an ordinary machine. No special hardware today.

11Any slide · the questions that decide the room

Proof and trust

The slides carry no accuracy figures, by choice. Give these only when asked, and give them exactly.

11.1How accurate is it?★
Say

There is no single number, and I would distrust anyone who gave you one. We report each part the way it was tested. Two matter most.

For landslides: take one place that had a landslide and one that did not. It puts them the right way round about 6 times in 7, on ground it never saw.

For a gauged river: when it says 20%, about 20% happen.

Careful

Never “it is X% accurate”. Never a landslide level as a percentage.

11.2Has it predicted a real event?★
Say

Not live, and I will not claim it. We replayed past years to see what it would have said, and it held up. The flood forecast began its live record on 21 September, as the monsoon ended. The next monsoon is the real proof, and every forecast until then is on record.

11.3What is your false-alarm rate?
Say

For rivers it can be counted, and it is on the track record: three so far, all from one faulty reading.

For landslides I cannot give an honest one. If we flag a hillside and nothing is reported, either nothing happened or a slope failed where nobody wrote it down. Landslides here are heavily under-reported. Any figure would be made up.

11.4Who decides when to warn people?★
Say

The authority, always. We give a forecast, which is information. A warning is an instruction, with cost and public trust attached, and it belongs to the body that carries that responsibility. What we owe them is a clear picture, and honesty about how sure we are.

The logic
  • Both modules say on screen that they do not replace official warnings.
11.5What if it is wrong and someone is hurt?
Say

It will be wrong sometimes. Every forecast is. That is why it never says “safe”, never shows more precision than the data has, labels every number, and leaves the decision with the authority. Today most of this ground has no daily forecast at all. This is better than nothing, and honest about what it is not.

11.6What happens after a miss?
Say

It goes on the record like everything else, and becomes one of the examples the next version learns from. The misses are the most useful examples we will ever get.

11.7What are its weak spots?★
Say

Three, and none is hidden. It has not been through a live monsoon yet. The landslide timing rests on few dated landslides, so that half is a careful rule, and its forecasts cannot be graded until dated records come in. And it runs from one machine today.

Only if asked

If a technical listener digs, there are three more, each already written in our own notes.

A river already sitting above its line can be given too low a chance for the next two or three days. We have cut this sharply. It is not gone. Warnings for a river that has not yet risen are not affected.

High ground is under-warned for landslides, because fewer landslides have been mapped where surveyors rarely go.

Away from a gauge, the flood figure is an approximate outlook, and it is tagged that way.

Careful

Never say the high-river weakness is fixed.

11.8Why should a government trust a startup with this?
Say

Not on trust. On evidence. Start small: one district or one valley, one season, every forecast on record. Then judge us on the score.

11.9Has anyone outside checked it?
Say

Not yet. The method and testing pages are inside the app for anyone to read, and we would welcome a technical review.

Careful

No claims of certification, published papers, awards or endorsements.

11.10What have you tried that failed?
Say

Several things, and cheaply. Dating old landslides from satellite photographs: beaten by monsoon cloud. Learning landslide timing from a handful of dated events: it lost to the simple rule. Forecasting how big a landslide will be: the data could not support it. We test the risky idea in hours before building for weeks, and we keep the failures on record.

Three directions: more hazards, more places, more users. Each one has a planned part. Keep the dashed things dashed.

12.1How hard is it to add a hazard?

A new hazard brings three things: its where question, its when question, and a record of past events to learn from and be checked against. Everything else is already there. The record is the slow part, not the code.

12.2And a new state, or another country?★

The satellite inputs cover the world, so the same steps run anywhere. What does not travel is the local part: that place’s own records, and its own testing. We would not point a model trained here at another state and call it a forecast. Today everything is set up for Arunachal. Moving it is designed, not done.

Careful

Do not say “it works for any state”. Do not name the next state.

12.3Which hazard is next, and when?

Soil erosion is next, and it is being designed. Forest fire, riverbank erosion and drought are on the list after it.

Careful

No dates and no place.

12.4Why is my hazard not on the list?

The list shows the ones the backbone already holds most of the data for. Others fit the same shape, as long as there is a where, a when, and a record to check against. Tell us the one that matters to you. That is the first of the three things we ask for.

Say

A way for other systems to read from the two shelves directly, without opening our app: the lined-up data, or the forecasts. So a department’s own dashboard, or another company’s product, can use them. It is planned. It comes after the pipeline runs fully by itself, because a feed of stale data is worse than no feed.

If they go further
Can all of the data be shared onward?

13Slide 12 · home page part 5

Stronger with time: the loop and the three steps

This is where the questions about competitors, records and sensors come. Every limit has a step that lifts it: name the step.

13.1Why does checking forecasts make it better, and not just prouder?
Say

Because a checked forecast is a new example: this place, this day, this rain, this result. Dated, located examples are the scarcest thing in this field. The system produces them every day, simply by running.

13.2“Code can be copied.” So what stops a bigger player?★
Say

They could build the pipeline. The hard parts are local: getting these sources to fit, knowing which official layer cannot be trusted and why, and seasons of forecasts checked on these rivers and these slopes. Money buys computers. It cannot buy last monsoon.

13.3Who else does this? Who are your competitors?★
Say

The closest are sensor companies in the western Himalaya. They put instruments on a slope already known to be dangerous and watch it minute by minute. That is useful, and different. We scan the whole state without hardware and say which slopes and rivers deserve an instrument. The two fit together.

Only if asked

For your own ears: one grew out of IIT Mandi, with instruments on about 60 slopes in Himachal and Uttarakhand, published work and a real record. A newer one is in Dehradun. Neither covers a whole state without hardware, and the first has no flood product.

Careful

Never run a competitor down.

13.4The “ask” step: which records, exactly?★
Say

Things offices already keep for their own work. Road-closure registers: every landslide that shut a road, with a date. Rain gauge records. Damage and relief registers from district offices. On the flood side: river cross-sections, dam release schedules, and any gauges not yet public.

The logic
  • Road-closure registers matter most. A dated landslide is the one thing the landslide forecast lacks, and those registers hold years of them.
  • “Letters, not money”: the records exist. Getting them takes a request, not a budget.
If they go further
Does it work without them?
Yes. It runs today without any of them. They make a working system finer. They are not a condition for starting.
13.5What happens to records we hand over?
Say

They keep their source and their terms attached, like every file in the backbone. They go to a holding area first and are checked before they touch anything live. How they may be used and shown is agreed with your office in writing, beforehand.

Careful

The holding area is designed, not built. Agree the wording on terms with the CEO first.

13.6The “paid” step: what would sensors do?
Say

On a slope: instruments that measure tilt, cracks widening and water in the ground. Most slopes creep before they fail, so hours of warning become possible. On a river: a level sensor upstream. And the backbone says where they should go, so the money lands on the right slopes.

If they go further
What does a site cost?
Low-cost sites in this field run in the region of a lakh each, against crores for imported systems. Our own costing would come with a pilot.
Will you make hardware?
No. Sensors are an input to the platform. We would partner for the hardware. What we bring is knowing where it should go, and what to do with the readings.
And drones?
A drone gives one hillside in fine detail, again and again, so change can be measured. Planned, for the sites the backbone flags.
Is there a landslide that sensors cannot help with?
Sudden flows and rockfalls. They give no sign beforehand. In the state’s own record they are a small minority. Most are slides, and slides do creep.
Careful

Give no firm price.

13.7Can people on the ground feed it: officers, residents?
Say

That is part of the loop we are building towards: field and citizen reports, checked before they count, each one a new dated example. Today there is a simple form for reporting a flood. A proper intake with checking is planned.

Careful

The report form is in our own copy of the app, not on the public link.

13.8Does each step up make it more accurate?
Say

Mostly it makes it finer and earlier. From an area, to a village, to one slope. From days, to hours. That is what makes it easier to act on.
