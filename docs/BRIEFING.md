## How to use this page

The page has three parts.

- **Part A follows the landing page, top to bottom.** The presenter scrolls the app's home page; you scroll this page beside it. Each section opens with the words to say, then the detail behind them, then the questions that usually come at that point.
- **Part B is the demo and the screen guide.** What each item on the app's screens means, in the order it sits on screen.
- **Part C is APSAC.** Who they are, what we use of theirs, what we ask, what we offer, every question they are likely to ask, the numbers card, the limits card, and what to agree before the meeting.

Every answer has the same labels.

- **Say:** the answer, in words you can speak as written.
- **Detail:** the exact figure and where it comes from, for a scientist who asks.
- **Careful:** what not to say, or where to stop.

**Six lines to fall back on**, when a question surprises you:

1. **Do the hard part once.** Any question about why a backbone, cost, speed, the next product.
2. **A product is a question: where, times when.** Any question about how a forecast works, or a new hazard.
3. **Written down before, checked after.** Any question about accuracy, trust or proof.
4. **Every number says what it is, and says so when unsure.** Any question about a figure on screen.
5. **An area and days today. Finer with records. One slope with sensors.** Any question that starts with "can it…". Give the limit and what lifts it.
6. **We give the forecast. The warning stays with the authority.** Any question about responsibility.

**How to answer anything, in four moves:** say the true thing in one line · give the number and where it is from · say what it does not mean · stop. Silence after a good answer is fine.

**Where the line is**

| Say freely | Only when asked | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| The idea, the five steps, the rules we keep. What each product asks, gives and reads. How to read the screen. 46 sources, 8.2 million squares, 26 years. ## A1 · The problem: the facts exist, in pieces

*On screen, section 01: "The facts already exist. They do not fit together." Six sources, six shapes (ground height every 30 m, soil every 250 m, rain every 11 km, rock types as outlines, rivers and roads as lines, river gauges as single points). Four tiles: Data in pieces · Starts from zero · Maps go stale · Nobody keeps score. Result: "Static maps. One-off studies. Pilots that never scale."*

> **Say:** Everything needed to see a landslide or a flood coming has already been measured. It sits with a dozen agencies, in a dozen shapes. The height of the ground comes every 30 metres. Soil every 250 metres. Rain every 11 kilometres. Rock types come as outlines, rivers and roads as lines, river gauges as single points. None of it lines up with the next. So four things break. The data is in pieces. Every project starts from zero. A map made once goes stale, because rain and rivers change every day. And nobody keeps score: forecasts are not written down and checked. The result is what you see everywhere: static maps, one-off studies, and pilots that never scale.

**Detail:**

- **In pieces.** The sources sit with NASA and the European satellite programmes, GSI, ISRO and NRSC, APSAC itself, the Central Water Commission, the national water-data portal and others. Some sit behind portals that look closed and are not.
- **Starts from zero.** A landslide study spends months lining the data up, publishes one map, and stops. The flood study next door does the same work again.
- **Goes stale.** The ground barely changes. Rain and rivers change daily. A one-off map looks the same in January and in August.
- **Nobody keeps score.** Without a written-down forecast and a check afterwards, nothing learns, and nobody can prove a forecast was any good.
- **Our own lesson.** On our first forecast the model was the small part. Finding, checking and lining up the data was most of the work, and all of it was reusable. That is where the backbone idea came from.

### Questions that come at this point

#### Q: If the data is all public, what is actually yours?

> **Say:** Three things. The assembly: 46 sources made to fit one map. The upkeep: it is fetched and checked every day, not once. And the checked history: every forecast written down and graded. Anyone can download the same files. Very few will do the rest. And nobody can download seasons of checked local history.

**Careful:** Never "we own unique data". Most inputs are public. Ours is the assembly, the upkeep and the checked record.

#### Q: Why has nobody done this already? Not even the government?

> **Say:** Pieces exist: hazard maps, global flood models, sensor companies, department portals. Each agency does its own part well. What does not exist yet for this state is all of it joined, running daily, and checked. Joining them is nobody's job. That is the gap.

#### Q: There are already landslide maps. How is this different?

> **Say:** Those maps answer **where**. They look the same in January and in August. We add **when**: today's rain and river against that place's own history, for the next seven days. And we keep score. We use the official maps as a cross-check, never as an input.

**Careful:** With APSAC in the room, their own zonation maps are among "those maps". Say "they answer where, and that is the base everything stands on".

#### Q: Do you have the right to use all this data?

> **Say:** Almost all of it is published as open data, and each source's terms of use are recorded with the file. At this stage we use it to build and to demonstrate. Before anything is sold or passed on, each source's terms are checked again, and permission is asked where a source needs it. For APSSDI that is one of our asks today.

**Careful:** Never say a licence, a permission or a partnership is secured.

## A2 · The idea: do the hard part once

*On screen, section 02: "Do the hard part once. Build everything on top." Four steps: Collect every useful source · Check before trusting it · Line up on one shared map · Keep current every day. "8.2 million squares, each the size of a football field." A stack of layers (rain, rivers and gauges, soil and land cover, ground shape) with a line straight down through one square. "Built on top: SlideSense, FloodSense, next."*

> **Say:** So we do the hard part once. Collect every useful source. Check it before trusting it. Line it up on one shared map. And keep it current every day. Picture the state as a sheet of graph paper. Each square is 100 metres across, about the size of a football field, and there are 8.2 million of them. Every source, whatever shape it arrived in, becomes one more fact about each square. So any square can be read straight down: how steep, what soil, what rock, how far from a river, how much rain. Products are built on top of that.

**Detail:**

- **The grid:** 8.2 million squares of 100 m across Arunachal (83,743 km²), on one projection. Ground height comes from the 30 m Copernicus elevation model; slopes are worked out at 25 m and summarised per square.
- **34 facts per square** today: ground shape, drainage, soil, rock, land cover, and distances to rivers, roads and fault lines.
- **26 years of daily rain** for every place, from satellite (NASA IMERG, from 1 June 2000, about 11 km).
- **How a shape becomes a fact.** A coarse layer such as rain is shared by every square under it. An outline (rock types) becomes "which rock am I on". A line (river, road, fault) becomes "how far to the nearest one". A point (a gauge) belongs to the river stretch it measures.

### Questions that come at this point

#### Q: Why 100 metres? Why not finer?

> **Say:** It is a balance. Much finer, and there are close to a hundred million squares, so every test takes days and we try fewer ideas. Much coarser, and a cliff and a meadow get averaged into one gentle slope. 100 metres keeps the real slope visible and still lets us work fast.

**Careful:** We work on 100 m squares. The answer is honest for an area, a town or a stretch of road, not for one house.

#### Q: Your map is 100 metres but your rain is 11 kilometres. Isn't that false precision?

> **Say:** Two halves. The ground half is 100 metres, from terrain, soil and cover. The rain half is at rain scale: about 11 km in the 26-year history, and about 22 km in the live forecast. The fine texture you see on the map is the ground, not the weather, and the app says so. We also never judge rain in raw millimetres. We ask how unusual it is for that place, which holds up much better on broad rain data.

**Detail:** This is the first question an APSAC scientist is likely to ask. The live rain is read at 219 points across the state from Open-Meteo. The full set of 892 points exists; fetching all of them in the daily cloud run is a budget step, not a physics limit. A cloudburst that falls between two points is a physics limit: it needs a ground or radar rain reading in that valley.

#### Q: How do you know the shape of ground nobody has surveyed?

> **Say:** Satellites have measured it. There is a height map of the whole world made by radar in orbit, with a reading every 30 metres. From height alone you can work out how steep a slope is, which way it faces, and where water will run. That is why a small team can cover a whole state. For one particular slope you want finer: a drone or a ground survey. That belongs to the paid step, on chosen spots.

#### Q: Is this a GIS? Are you a GIS company?

> **Say:** GIS is the craft of working with map data, and we use it everywhere: it is how the sources get lined up. But a GIS is a tool a person uses to look at maps. This is a system that keeps the maps current by itself and turns them into a forecast every morning.

## A3 · How it works: one pipeline, and a way back

*On screen, section 03: five steps (01 Collect · 02 Keep · 03 Line up · 04 Shelve · 05 Serve), and a return arrow: "Every forecast is written down, then checked against what really happened. Comes back in as data." Three figures: 46 sources lined up · 8.2 million squares on the shared map · 26 years of daily rain, for every place. Then "Why you can trust it · rules we keep in mind": six tiles. Then the Data Backbone strip: "See it for yourself: every source, on record. Every layer, on one map."*

> **Say:** It is one pipeline, and a way back. Collect: a script fetches each source, from satellite archives, government map servers, river gauges, and a live weather feed every day. Keep: the original file is stored untouched, with a record of where it came from. Line up: it is fitted to the shared map. Shelve: the result goes on one of two shelves, ready-to-use data, or answers, the forecasts. Serve: products read from there. And the way back: every forecast is written down before the day, then checked against what really happened, and that comes back in as new data. Today that is 46 sources, 8.2 million squares, and 26 years of daily rain for every place.

**The rules we keep in mind** (the page shows six; there are more)

| Rule | What it means | An example to give |
|---|---|---|
| **One map for everything** | Every source becomes one more column of facts about the same squares | Two products can never disagree about where a place is |
| **Keep the original** | Raw files are never edited. Each carries its source, terms of use, link, the date fetched and a fingerprint | Any number traces back to its file. If a source goes offline we lose freshness, not the system |
| **Check before trusting** | Every new source is tested against something independent | The national flood layer put 70.5% of its "flooded" pixels on slopes over 15°. One wrong river reading once looked like a 52% flood |
| **No hazard built in** | Nothing in the backbone says "landslide" or "flood". A product brings its own question | That is what made the second product cheap |
| **Two shelves** | One holds base data to build on; the other holds forecasts to decide with | A road planner wants the first, an officer on duty the second |
| **Close the loop** | Every forecast is written down before the day and graded after it | 9,408 river forecasts written down since 21 September; 4,784 already checked (5 Nov) |

More rules, if asked: test on ground the model has never seen; write the pass mark down before running a test; label every number with what kind it is; say so when unsure; never tune a forecast on the data used to judge it; keep failed experiments on record.

**Careful:** Never say "six rules". Say "rules we keep in mind".

**Follow one file through** (the example to give):

> **Say:** Take the height of the ground. It arrives as satellite tiles. We keep those untouched, with a note of where they came from. Then it is fitted to the squares and turned into steepness, the way each slope faces, how water drains, and how high each square sits above its river. That goes on the shelf. The landslide forecast reads the steepness. The flood forecast reads the height above the river. The erosion module reads the slope again. One file, three products.

**Detail — the three figures:**

| Figure | Exactly | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 46 | sources held, about 344 GB of original files (14,075 files). **20 more** are checked live and ready to pull, so the app shows 66 with "46 held · 20 ready to pull" | webapp/assets/backbone/catalogue.json, 4 Nov |
| 8.2 million | 100 m squares inside the state, 34 facts each | the shared grid |
| 26 years | daily satellite rain for every place, 1 June 2000 to today, no gaps | NASA IMERG archive |

**Careful:** Older pages and the 23 Oct APSAC prep say "29 sources, 308 GB". The live count is 46 held plus 20 ready. Never call a ready source data we hold.

### Questions that come at this point

#### Q: What does "keep the original" give you?

> **Say:** Three things. Any number on any screen traces back to the file it came from. If we find a mistake in our own processing, we rebuild from the original instead of patching. And if a source goes offline, we lose freshness, not the system.

#### Q: What if a source stops, or a feed is down on the day?

> **Say:** The system keeps running. We hold our own copy of everything historical. If today's rain or a gauge feed is late, the forecast is less fresh, and the screen says so. It never fills a gap with a guess.

**Detail:** A real case on screen today: the gauges-reporting tile. On 5 November, 17 of the 32 gauges reported. All 14 small-river gauges (NWDP) are offline at their source, and one CWC gauge too. The screen shows each as offline, with the date of its last reading.

#### Q: "Written down before, checked after." How, exactly?

> **Say:** Each morning the forecast is saved to a log before the day it speaks about. When the day has passed, the real reading is fetched and set beside what we said. For rivers that is simple: the gauge tells us whether the river reached its line. The running score is on the track record panel in the app. The log only grows. Even a wrong forecast stays in it.

**Detail:** One morning in September a faulty river reading made us raise three false warnings. They are still in the record, on purpose: a record you can tidy is not a record.

#### Q: Is it automatic? Where does it run?

> **Say:** Yes. Every morning at half past eight, India time, a cloud job fetches the rain and the rivers, makes both forecasts, saves them, and grades the earlier ones. It runs in a data centre in Mumbai, because the water commission's gauge site answers only from inside India. The public app picks up the new forecast by itself. Since 4 November a second small job also saves every river reading, every hour.

**Detail:** Moved to the cloud on 20 October after the office PC missed four mornings. Modal, region ap-south (Mumbai). The app itself runs on Streamlit Community Cloud. Still by hand: refreshing the slower sources, and checking the run if it fails. **Honest weak spot:** nobody is alerted automatically if the morning run fails. A small alert on the job lifts that.

#### Q: What are the "two shelves"?

> **Say:** One shelf holds the ready-to-use data: the map and all its facts, the same for every product. The other holds the answers: the forecasts. Different people want different shelves. Someone planning a road wants the base data. An officer on duty wants the forecast.

#### Q: How big is all this? What computing does it need?

> **Say:** About 344 GB of original files, most of it satellite radar scenes. What the app needs is boiled down to a few megabytes, which is why it opens quickly on a phone. The heavy work is done beforehand and stored. The daily run takes a few minutes on an ordinary cloud machine. No special hardware.

## A4 · Running today: a product is a question

*On screen, section 04: "A product is a question asked of the backbone." WHERE can it happen (the ground, barely changes) × WHEN is something setting it off (rain, rivers, change daily) = Forecast, place by place, the next seven days. Three rows: SlideSense = weak ground × unusual rain for that place. FloodSense = ground that water can reach × rain upstream and the river's level. Soil erosion (next) = slope, soil and plant cover × how hard the rain falls. Then the two product cards with screenshots, "The saving" table, "2 files", and the two "More about" panels.*

> **Say:** A product is a question asked of the backbone. Every hazard has the same shape. Where can it happen: that is about the ground, which barely changes. When is something setting it off: that is rain and rivers, which change daily. Multiply the two and you get a forecast, place by place, for the next seven days. The landslide forecast asks: weak ground, times rain that is unusual for that place. The flood forecast asks: ground that water can reach, times rain upstream and the river's own level. Soil erosion, the next one, asks: slope, soil and plant cover, times how hard the rain falls.

**Why multiply, not add:** you need both at once. A weak slope in a dry week is quiet. Heavy rain on firm, flat ground does little. If either half is near zero, the answer should be near zero.

**Why "unusual for that place":** some parts of the state get several times the rain of others. 150 mm in three days is ordinary in the wet foothills and rare on a dry northern ridge. One rain limit for the whole state would alarm the wet parts all monsoon and never warn the dry ones. So each place is compared with its own 26 years.

### SlideSense, the landslide forecast

> **Say:** SlideSense gives a seven-day landslide outlook for any town, village, point or road in the state. The where half learned from the state's own record: about 37,000 landslides mapped inside Arunachal by GSI, NRSC and APSAC. It found the combinations of ground that keep turning up where slopes failed, and scores every square the same way. The when half asks how unusual the last three and seven days of rain are for that exact place. The map shows five levels, Very Low to Very High. A level is a rank, not a percentage: High means look here first. Beside it we show a measured figure: at High, landslides have been about three times as common as usual; at Very High, about ten times.

**Detail:**

| What | Figure | Where from |
|---|---|---|
| Mapped landslides it learned from | 37,788 outlines → 91,610 squares. GSI 26,459 + NRSC Bhuvan 2014 and 2017 + APSAC SILAAS 2023 (11,329 together) | metrics.json |
| How it learns where | Gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM) on 34 facts per square | — |
| How well it ranks place | 0.859 ± 0.016 (AUC) on 50 km blocks it never saw: shown one place that slid and one that did not, it puts them the right way round about 6 times in 7 | metrics.json |
| Terrain and drainage alone | 0.839, 97.7% of the full model. So it is not just "finding roads" | MODEL_SUSCEPTIBILITY.md |

**What is on its screens:** Forecast (seven-day outlook for any place, with the week card and the day boxes); Roads (kilometres of main road at High or above, and any route between two towns); Susceptibility (how weak the ground is, the same every day); the one-page report; Evidence; Model and Validation.

**Made for:** district disaster management, roads and public works, power and telecom lines, tour and transport operators.

**Careful:** "It is an outlook for an area, days ahead. It does not watch a single slope, and it does not replace official warnings." Never give a landslide level as a percentage. Never say "safe".

### FloodSense, the flood forecast

> **Say:** FloodSense gives a seven-day flood outlook for any place, and follows the river gauges on the state's rivers. The where half asks how easily water reaches a spot: how high it sits above its river, how big that river is, how closed-in the valley is. That was learned from past floods confirmed by satellite radar, a global flood database and river gauges. The when half reads the rain here and the rain upstream that will arrive later, and at 32 river gauges it also reads the river's own level over the past week. At a gauged river it gives the chance the river reaches its danger line on each of the next seven days. And that chance means what it says: on past years, when it said about 20%, about 20% happened. It runs by itself every morning and grades itself against the real river.

**Detail:**

| What | Figure | Where from |
|---|---|---|
| River gauges read | 32: 18 Central Water Commission + 14 national water-data portal (NWDP) small rivers. 7 have official danger levels; elsewhere the line is the river's own wettest 2% of days, labelled as such | live snapshot |
| Verified floods it learned from | 2,180: 1,016 from the Global Flood Database, 1,153 from a radar test we built and checked, 125 from gauges | snapshot method.record |
| The radar test | Correct on 97.8% of known flood-free dates before it was trusted. The old radar screen passed 97–100% of flood-free dates, so it was thrown out | snapshot method.radar |
| Timing, on floods it never saw | 0.896 (AUC) on 43 Arunachal floods, 2023–25 (1,035 days). Older docs say 0.879: same test, earlier model | snapshot method.timing |
| "20% means 20%" (calibration) | Said 10–25%: averaged 15.9%, happened 15.4%. Said 25–50%: averaged 36.7%, happened 37.9%. Said 75–100%: 91.0% vs 90.7% | snapshot method.gauge |
| Warning at 25%, one day ahead (replayed) | Of floods at gauges, 61% were warned; of warnings, 68% came true. On rain alone it caught only 10% | snapshot method.gauge |
| Level line | The river's expected daily high, today and tomorrow, at 17 CWC gauges. Replayed miss 0.13 m a day ahead (0.17 m if the river "stayed put"), 0.22 m two days ahead (0.26 m) | snapshot method.level |
| Level line, live | 784 checks since it went live: average miss 0.08 m against 0.11 m for "stayed put". Its range is built to hold 8 days in 10 and held 85% | snapshot track_record, 5 Nov |
| Track record, live | 9,408 river forecasts written down since 21 September; 4,784 checked. No river reached its line; on average it said 0.13%. 3 warnings, all from one faulty reading on 29 Sep | snapshot track_record, 5 Nov |
| Soil wetness | Helps on flat ground, read 16 days back. Tested: no help for landslides | snapshot method.soil |
| "Last 3 days" view | Every gauge reading, hourly, saved since 4 Nov. CWC reads hourly in its flood season, 3 times a day (08:00, 13:00, 18:00) from 1 Nov | gauge-hourly job |

**What is on its screens:** Live forecast (chance of a flood near any place, for the week and day by day); River gauges (each river against its line, the level line, the "Last 3 days" view); Flood-prone ground; Catchments (the rain upstream of every place); Track record; the one-page report; How to read this forecast.

**Made for:** district disaster management, water resources and flood control, towns along the rivers, hydropower operators.

**Careful:** The "near here" percentage for a town is an **outlook**: approximate, tagged so on screen. It failed its strict calibration test, so we never call it a forecast, and in absolute terms its area figures run about 4 times high over Arunachal (the ranking is good). "20% means 20%" is true at gauged rivers, on past years. It has not been through a live monsoon yet.

### Questions that come at this point

#### Q: How does it know where the ground is weak?

> **Say:** It learned from the state's own record. Tens of thousands of landslides have been mapped here, by GSI, by NRSC, and by APSAC's own SILAAS survey. We show the computer those places, and many places with no landslide, with the facts about each: steepness, the way the slope faces, soil, rock, cover, drainage. It finds the combinations that keep turning up where slopes failed. Then it scores every square in the state the same way.

**Keep back:** which facts matter most in order, how the no-landslide places are chosen, and the settings.

#### Q: How does it decide when?

> **Say:** From rain. For each place it takes the rain of the last three and seven days, including the forecast days, and asks how unusual that is for that exact place, against 26 years. Weak ground and unusually wet: the level goes up.

**Detail:** The rain half is a plain rule, not a learned model. Only about 90 landslides in Arunachal have a recorded day; when we tried to learn the timing from so few, the fitted model lost to the simple rule. We switch when the dated record justifies it.

#### Q: What does "High" mean? Is it a chance?

> **Say:** A rank, not a percentage. High means: look here first. Beside it we show a measured figure. At High, landslides have been about three times as common as usual. At Very High, about ten times. Nobody has an honest percentage for a landslide: the records say where slopes failed, not how many held, and many landslides are never reported. A percentage would be invented.

**Careful:** The "3 times" and "10 times" rest on 84 dated landslides. Always say "about".

#### Q: Does Very Low mean safe?

> **Say:** No. We never use the word safe. Very Low means landslides have been rare on ground like this, in rain like this, about a fifth of the usual. Rare is not never.

#### Q: How far ahead, and how exact in place?

> **Say:** Seven days, with the first days firmer than the last; days four to seven are drawn dashed. In place: an area, a town, a stretch of road. Not a single slope, and not an hour. For one slope, tomorrow afternoon, you need instruments on that slope. Most slopes creep before they fail, and the creep can be measured. That is the paid step, on chosen slopes.

**Careful:** Sudden flows and rockfalls give no sign beforehand. In GSI's Arunachal record most failures are slides that creep (96.6%); flows and falls are about 3%.

#### Q: What about landslides not caused by rain: earthquakes, road cutting, blasting?

> **Say:** Today the trigger is rain, which sets off most landslides here. Road cutting shows up in part, because distance to a road is one of the facts about the ground. Earthquake shaking would be a second trigger; we do not forecast earthquakes themselves, nobody can. Construction and blasting records are on our ask list.

#### Q: What does 20% on a river mean?

> **Say:** The chance that the river reaches its danger line on that day. And it is meant literally: over past years, on the days it said about 20%, the river reached its line about one time in five.

#### Q: Whose danger levels?

> **Say:** The Central Water Commission's, where they are published: seven of our 32 gauges. Elsewhere we use the level that river reaches only on its wettest few days, and the screen labels it as such.

#### Q: What about rivers and streams with no gauge? Flash floods?

> **Say:** They get the outlook from rain and terrain: an approximate chance of a flood in that area, tagged approximate. A cloudburst over one small valley is the hardest case: the rain forecast we read is too broad to see it, and it is over in hours. What we give is the wider picture: these valleys are primed this week. Warning by the hour needs weather radar or a sensor upstream.

#### Q: Dam releases? Glacial lakes?

> **Say:** Not in it today. A release from a dam is a decision, not weather. With the operators' release schedules it can be added, and that is on our ask list. Glacial-lake bursts are a separate hazard with their own data. We have no lake layer yet; APSAC's glacial-lake atlas is one of our asks.

#### Q: Can it show how deep the water will be, and which houses?

> **Say:** Not today. It says how likely water is to reach a spot if a flood comes. Depth needs the river's cross-section and a detailed survey of the ground: river cross-sections from the water commission, and drone or LiDAR terrain. With those, yes, for chosen towns.

#### Q: Is this AI? What kind?

> **Say:** Yes: machine learning. The computer is shown past events and the facts about each place, and finds the pattern itself. The kind we use is built for tables of facts: gradient-boosted decision trees, many small sets of yes-or-no rules, each correcting the last. It is the standard choice for tables, it retrains in minutes, and it can show which facts drove each answer.

#### Q: Why not deep learning?

> **Say:** We use the tool the data justifies. For where, we have about 37,000 mapped slides and 34 facts per square: on tables, trees are still the most accurate and can explain themselves. Our real shortage is in time: about 90 dated landslides. Deep learning does not fix a shortage of examples. Deep models are in the plan, for images and long sequences, and a model joins only if it makes the whole panel better on data it never saw.

#### Q: How do you stop it fooling you?

> **Say:** Three habits. Test only on regions and years it never saw: we hide whole 50-kilometre blocks while it learns. Write the pass mark down before running the test. And add a check a score cannot fake: look at the map. Once, a version scored better on paper and put its highest flood risk on lakes at the tops of mountains. We threw it out.

#### Q: Is it a black box? Can you explain one forecast?

> **Say:** No. For any place the screen shows the two halves apart: how weak the ground is, and how unusual the rain is, with the rain in millimetres. An officer can see why the level is what it is. Behind that, the method can list which facts pushed a score up or down.

#### Q: Only two files for a whole flood forecast? Really?

> **Say:** For the first working flood module, yes. Everything else was reused. Since then it has grown its own parts: the river gauges, the verified flood record, the daily check. And those went into the backbone, so the next product gets them without building them.

### Questions that come at this point

#### Q: How hard is it to add a hazard? A new state?

> **Say:** A new hazard brings three things: its where question, its when question, and a record of past events to learn from and be checked against. Everything else is already there. The record is the slow part, not the code. A new place needs its own records and its own testing: we would never point a model trained here at another state and call it a forecast.

**Detail (team only):** the soil-erosion module was moved to a second state, Assam, in about four weeks: a region switch, Assam's grid and outline, and SoilSense on top. **Careful:** Do not say "it works for any state". In anything shown to others, do not name the next state.

#### Q: What is the open feed?

> **Say:** A way for other systems to read from the two shelves directly, without opening our app. So a department's own dashboard, or APSSDI, could show the forecasts. It is planned. It comes after the whole pipeline runs by itself, because a feed of stale data is worse than none.

#### Q: Why does checking forecasts make it better, and not just prouder?

> **Say:** Because a checked forecast is a new example: this place, this day, this rain, this result. Dated, located examples are the scarcest thing in this field. The system produces them every day, simply by running.

#### Q: The "ask" step: which records, exactly?

> **Say:** Things offices already keep for their own work. Road-closure registers: every landslide that shut a road, with a date. Rain-gauge records. Damage and relief registers from district offices. On the flood side: river cross-sections, dam release schedules, and danger levels not yet published. From APSAC: SILAAS records with field dates, finer statewide ground layers, and their hazard maps as benchmarks. It runs today without any of them. They make a working system finer; they are not a condition for starting.

#### Q: The "paid" step: what would sensors do?

> **Say:** On a slope: instruments that measure tilt, cracks widening and water in the ground. Most slopes creep before they fail, so hours of warning become possible. On a river: a level sensor upstream. And the backbone says where they should go, so the money lands on the right slopes. We would partner for the hardware. What we bring is knowing where it should go, and what to do with the readings.

**Careful:** No firm price. "Low-cost sites in this field run in the region of a lakh each" is the most to say, and only if pressed.

#### Q: What do you want from us?

> **Say:** One of three things. Tell us a hazard that worries you. Name a place you are responsible for. Or point us to records sitting in a cabinet. Any one of them is enough to begin. From APSAC specifically, Part C has the list.

### The model

#### Q: What is the method?

> **Say:** Where: gradient-boosted decision trees on 34 facts per square, learned from 37,788 mapped slides. When: how unusual the last three and seven days of rain are at that exact spot. The forecast is weak ground times unusual rain.

#### Q: Did you test on random points? Neighbours leak, so the score is inflated.

> **Say:** No. We tested on 50-kilometre blocks the model never saw: 0.859, plus or minus 0.016. Random splits would score higher, which is exactly why we do not use them.

**Careful:** Do not quote one decimal more than you have.

#### Q: How did you pick the no-landslide places? Random ones on gentle ground make it easy.

> **Say:** We tested picking them only from steep ground: 0.860 against 0.862, no real difference here, because almost all of the state is steep. We choose them so the labels are valid, not for the score.

**Keep back:** the exact sampling rules.

#### Q: How accurate is it?

> **Say:** There is no single number, and I would distrust anyone who gave one. Each part is reported the way it was tested. Landslide place: on ground it never saw, it puts a landslide place above a quiet one about six times in seven. Landslide timing: 0.77 on 72 dated slides, a wide band because there are so few. Places and days at High have had about three times the usual landslides, Very High about ten times. Flood timing: 0.90 on 43 Arunachal floods it never saw. And at a gauged river, 20% means 20%.

**Careful:** Never "it is X% accurate". 0.86 is a ranking score, not a percentage of right answers. If someone says "so it's 86% accurate", correct kindly: "Not quite: 0.86 is a ranking score. Show it a place that slid and one that didn't, on ground it never saw, and it picks the right one about 6 times in 7."

#### Q: Is it a probability?

> **Say:** For landslides, no: a rank and a "times the usual" figure. For floods at a gauged river, yes: the chance of reaching the danger line, and calibrated. For a town away from a gauge: an approximate outlook, tagged as such.

#### Q: Does it just find roads?

> **Say:** No. Terrain and drainage alone, ten inputs, give 0.839, 97.7% of the full model.

#### Q: Why didn't you use intensity–duration thresholds, like the literature?

> **Say:** We tried. On our data the intensity–duration line came out backwards. The total-rain line caught 89% of slides but fired 87 to 165 days a year, so it cannot warn. A per-place percentile is fairer: dry places are not silent and wet places are not always on alarm.

#### Q: How good is it in the high mountains?

> **Say:** Weaker, and we know why. At 2,000 to 4,000 metres, 42% of the land we model, it gives High or above to 10.5% of the land, but that band holds 20.3% of the known slides. Fewer slides get mapped where access is hard, so the model has seen fewer. Field-checked high-altitude slides from SILAAS go straight at that gap, and elevation-aware thresholds are the other fix.

#### Q: Your rain is 11 to 22 kilometres.

> **Say:** True. The fine detail on the map comes from the terrain, not the weather, and satellite rain is least reliable high up. Ground gauges fix that: seven CWC stations in the state send live three-hourly rain, and IMD's records are on our ask list.

#### Q: The flood area numbers look high.

> **Say:** They are, about four times high in absolute terms over Arunachal, though the ranking is good. The screen tags that figure as an approximate outlook. Better flood records and finer terrain bring it down; your flood zonation is the benchmark we would use.

#### Q: Flood depth from a 30 m elevation model?

> **Say:** We do not give depth, only the chance of water reaching a spot. Depth needs river cross-sections and drone or LiDAR terrain, where funded. Your team processes LiDAR and flies drones, so that is a natural joint piece.

**Careful:** "APSAC has LiDAR" is wrong: they process LiDAR; no scanner is listed. They fly camera drones.

## C9 · The limits card

Every limit with what lifts it. **Free** we can do ourselves; **Ask** needs a letter; **Paid** needs money. **Physics** limits have no key.

| Limit | Lifted by | Step | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live rain is about 22 km, not 11 | Fetch all 892 points in the daily cloud run; a paid weather plan for commercial use | Free | Budget |
| Landslide timing rests on ~90 dated slides; cannot be graded live | Dated road-closure logs (PWD, BRO, PMGSY); SILAAS dates | Ask | Budget |
| High ground is under-warned | More mapping where surveyors rarely go; SILAAS field records | Ask | Budget |
| The flood "near here" outlook is approximate | More verified flood records; people's reports | Ask | Budget |
| A river above its line can be told too low a chance | More high-water days to learn from; upstream gauges | Free | Budget |
| No flood depth, no "which houses" | CWC cross-sections (ask) and LiDAR or drone terrain (paid) | Paid | Budget |
| Districts are GADM's 18, not today's 28 | APSSDI's district layer | Ask | Budget |
| Monsoon cloud hides the ground from optical satellites | Radar instead | Free | Physics |
| A valley cloudburst can fall between rain points | Ground or radar rain in that valley | Paid | Physics |
| Not through a live monsoon yet | Time: the next monsoon | Free | Physics |
| Nobody is alerted if the daily run fails | A small alert on the cloud job | Free | Budget |
| Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial | A paid plan before a paying client | Paid | Budget |
| One slope's movement cannot be seen from data | Sensors on chosen slopes | Paid | Physics |
| Sudden flows and rockfalls give no sign beforehand | None: about 3% of the state's mapped failures | — | Physics |

**Careful:** Never say a free-step limit as if it were permanent. Name what lifts it.

| "We own unique data." | "The inputs are public. Ours is the assembly and the checked record." |

## C11 · Agree before the meeting

Nothing written in the project answers these. Agree one line each with the CEO and COO.

- **The offer to APSAC.** Which of C4 we commit to, and by when.

- **Terms for their records.** One sentence on how an office's records are held, used and shown.

- **Branding.** Whether a joint product carries APSAC's name, ours, or both.
